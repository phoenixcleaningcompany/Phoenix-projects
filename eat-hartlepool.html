Where to Eat in Hartlepool

Quick Answer

For a fast answer: Fish Face on Seaton Carew seafront for award-winning fish and chips a few steps from the North Sea, Glady's Vintage Tea Room for a 1940s afternoon tea on the same promenade, Portals on Hartlepool Marina for Italian and Mediterranean cooking with views of the historic docks, and The Fishermans Arms on the Headland for cask ale and live music in a one-room pub that has been pouring since 1868. Four moods, one compact coastal town.

Hartlepool does not have one food scene so much as several, layered street by street. This guide does something simple: it picks one genuine standout in each of four everyday categories and uses them as doorways into the wider town.

The Four Picks

Takeaway/Casual

Fish Face

36 The Front, Seaton Carew

Seaton Carew is Hartlepool's Edwardian seafront village, a mile of stuccoed townhouses and beach-facing businesses where the Quaker families of the North East once took their summer holidays. Fish Face sits directly on The Front, a family-run fish and chip restaurant and takeaway that has earned a string of regional awards for its chips and is ranked in the national top 50 by Fry Magazine. The menu runs further than a standard chippy: fresh cod and haddock in proper batter alongside scallops, lobster and gluten-free options. Inside there are tables for sit-down meals, and the fish is as fresh as the address suggests. The signature order is battered haddock, chippy chips and mushy peas, eaten with a sea view and a curry sauce on the side.

Glady's Vintage Tea Room

35 The Front, Seaton Carew

One door along from the seafront fryers, Glady's Vintage Tea Room inhabits a completely different register. The interior channels the 1940s: mismatched vintage china, bunting, table-cloths and a warm domestic clutter that earns the comparison to your grandparents' front room. Cakes and bakes are made in the upstairs kitchen, and the afternoon teas, served on tiered stands with finger sandwiches, scones and homemade patisserie, have drawn a loyal following since the tearoom opened. The venue passed to new owners Sharon and Stuart Greig in December 2024, who brought their background from their own catering business, and recent visitors in early 2026 have praised the atmosphere, the quality of the food and the friendly service. Open Wednesday to Sunday, 9am to 3pm; Mondays and Tuesdays closed.

Portals

Neptune House, Slake Terrace, Hartlepool Marina

Portals opened in November 2014 in Neptune House on the Marina, a modern building on the quayside close to HMS Trincomalee, the oldest warship still afloat in Europe. The menu draws on Italian and Mediterranean traditions: pasta, pizzas, fresh seafood boards and daily specials that reflect what is available on the coast. The terrace out front looks across the pontoons of the Marina, making it one of the better waterfront tables in the North East. Standout dishes praised by diners include chicken parmigiana, mussels and the house beef casserole. With over 1,700 reviews averaging 4.6 on Restaurant Guru and a large group event in December 2025 drawing enthusiastic responses, Portals is well established as the Marina's go-to independent. Book ahead for weekend evenings.

The Fishermans Arms

Southgate, the Headland, Hartlepool

The Headland is Hartlepool's original peninsula, a tight grid of Victorian streets running down to the Fish Quay, and The Fishermans Arms has stood on Southgate at the heart of it since 1868. Known to regulars simply as The Fish, it is everything a one-room pub should be: no television, no jukebox, no fruit machines, just changing cask ales from regional brewers including Maxim, Durham and Three Brothers, plus real ciders. Now privately owned following a full refurbishment that repositioned the bar, it is CAMRA-listed and holds a Cask Marque accreditation. Regular events include a Sunday quiz, open-mic nights, the Fisherfolk Folk Club on the first Monday of each month and a winter beer festival. It is also one of the Hartlepool Folk Fest venues, which takes place across the Headland each summer.

Hartlepool's Food Scene

Hartlepool's food map stretches between two distinct shorelines. Seaton Carew, the Edwardian village at the southern end of the bay, has been a seaside destination since Quaker families from Darlington and Stockton built its stuccoed villas in the early 1800s, and the seafront promenade today holds the town's best fish and chip shops and tearooms, with Fish Face and Glady's Vintage Tea Room among the most visited.

The Marina, reclaimed from the old docks in the 1990s, anchors the modern eating scene around Navigation Point and Slake Terrace. The same basin that once unloaded coal now shelters a mix of marina bars, the HMS Trincomalee museum and restaurants like Portals, which draw visitors as much for the waterfront setting as the food. The Headland, the old fishing quarter whose narrow streets run down to the Fish Quay, holds the town's oldest pub and a character that the Marina development did not erase.

The Teesside parmo is the region's cult dish: breaded chicken or pork topped with bechamel and melted cheddar, served in a pizza box, invented in Middlesbrough in 1958 by Greek-American chef Nicos Harris but claimed with equal enthusiasm across Hartlepool. Fish and chips is the enduring local staple, and the Seaton Carew seafront is where locals go to eat the best of them, walking the front with chips from a paper tray and a North Sea wind behind them.

Behind the Pass: The Kitchens That Keep Hartlepool Fed

Fryers, grills and pass after pass through a busy season put a commercial kitchen's extraction system - canopy, baffle filters, plenum, ductwork and fan - under real strain, drawing grease-laden vapour out of the building. On Hartlepool's seafront and in its Marina restaurants, friers run hard through long summer days and the grease load on canopies above fish ranges, chip pans and grill stations builds fast in the salt air.

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for a premises must assess and manage fire risk, and a grease-laden duct is one of the most serious risks in any catering building. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) reinforce the same duty of care, while an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) or a buildings insurer can ask to see documented proof the extraction system has been professionally cleaned.

What is TR19 Grease?

TR19 Grease is the UK industry standard for cleaning ductwork and extraction systems in commercial kitchens, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It defines how grease deposits are measured, the maximum thickness allowed before cleaning is due, and the post-clean certification a kitchen should hold - the benchmark insurers, fire assessors and EHOs look for.

A seaside kitchen earns its year in a few hot months. A clean extraction system is what keeps it trading through every one of them.

Industry Insight

The Cleaning Frequency Most Operators Get Wrong

Here is something many venue owners do not realise: under TR19 Grease, cleaning frequency is set by how many hours the kitchen actually cooks, not by how the canopy looks. A heavy-use kitchen running 12 to 16 hours a day should be cleaned roughly every three months. Moderate use of 6 to 12 hours points to every six months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours to every twelve.

The catch is that baffle filters can look clean while the plenum, the horizontal duct runs and the fan housing carry a heavy grease load that throttles airflow and lowers extract efficiency. A fire in that concealed ductwork spreads fast. That is why a dated TR19 certificate, kept in the fire logbook, is the document an insurer expects to see - and its absence at the point of a claim is a common reason cover is refused.

Planning Your Visit

The three main areas are connected by a short drive or a flat cycle ride: the Marina sits in the centre, Seaton Carew is a mile to the south, and the Headland juts out to the north. The Marina car parks are free outside peak hours, and the Seaton Carew promenade has beachfront parking. Hartlepool station, served by the Transpennine coastal line between Middlesbrough and Sunderland, is a short walk from the Marina.

A well-paced day runs from south to north. Start with breakfast or brunch at Glady's Tea Room on the Seaton Carew front, walk the beach, then move to the Marina for lunch or an afternoon meal at Portals with views across the pontoons. Cross to the Headland in the late afternoon to take in the Fish Quay streets and settle into The Fishermans Arms before the evening quiz or folk night begins. Save Fish Face for whenever hunger strikes: it opens from 11am and the fish is worth the queue.

  • Book Portals ahead for a weekend evening or large group visit
  • Glady's Tea Room is open Wednesday to Sunday only, 9am-3pm
  • The Fishermans Arms hosts the Fisherfolk Folk Club on the first Monday of each month
  • Walk the Seaton Carew promenade between Fish Face and Glady's - both are on The Front
  • Hartlepool station (Transpennine line) is a short walk from the Marina

What to Order and How to Do the Day

Order with intent. At Fish Face, battered haddock and chippy chips with mushy peas and a curry sauce, eaten on the seafront. At Glady's, the full afternoon tea for the tiered stand experience, or a cooked breakfast before the beach. At Portals, start with the mussels or chicken pate, and ask what the daily fish special is. At The Fishermans Arms, pick a locally brewed cask ale from the board - Maxim, Durham and Three Brothers rotate through - and stay for the quiz or the folk night.

Frequently Asked Questions

Hartlepool Food and Drink FAQ

Where can I get the best fish and chips in Hartlepool?

Fish Face at 36 The Front, Seaton Carew, is a family-run award-winning fish and chip restaurant and takeaway open daily from 11am. Ranked in the national top 50 by Fry Magazine, it serves fresh haddock and cod in proper batter alongside scallops and gluten-free options.

What is the best restaurant on Hartlepool Marina?

Portals at Neptune House, Slake Terrace, has been the Marina's go-to independent since opening in November 2014. It serves Italian and Mediterranean cooking with terrace views across the pontoons, and is consistently rated among the best restaurants in Hartlepool.

Is there a good tearoom or cafe in Hartlepool?

Glady's Vintage Tea Room at 35 The Front, Seaton Carew, serves homemade cakes, breakfasts and afternoon teas in a 1940s-themed room. Open Wednesday to Sunday 9am-3pm. The tiered afternoon teas are the most-praised item and booking is recommended at weekends.

What is the best pub on the Hartlepool Headland?

The Fishermans Arms on Southgate has been trading since 1868 and is the Headland's best-loved local: one room, no television, CAMRA-listed with Cask Marque accreditation and a changing range of ales from Durham, Maxim and Three Brothers. It holds regular folk nights and a Sunday quiz.

What is the local food speciality in Hartlepool?

Fish and chips is the enduring staple, with the Seaton Carew seafront its spiritual home. Hartlepool also sits in Teesside parmo country: the breaded chicken or pork topped with bechamel and melted cheddar, served in a pizza box, is the region's cult dish and widely available across town.

How do I get to Hartlepool without a car?

Hartlepool station is served by Transpennine Express on the coastal line between Middlesbrough and Sunderland, with connections to Durham and Newcastle. The station is a short flat walk from the Marina and close to the town centre. Seaton Carew has its own station one stop south.
