Where to Eat in Bedford

Quick Answer

For a fast answer: Chicken George Jnr on Tavistock Street for nationally award-winning fried chicken and wings, Coffee with Art on the High Street for a direct-trade flat white in Bedford's second-oldest shop front, Il Basilico on Mill Street for a family Italian whose pasta and pizza have made it the town's highest-rated restaurant, and the Wellington Arms on Wellington Street for eight handpumps of microbrewery real ale in CAMRA's Town Pub of the Year 2025. Four moods, one compact Bedfordshire market town.

Rather than list everywhere in Bedford, this guide names four places worth going out of your way for, one in each everyday category, and lets them point you towards the rest of the town.

The Four Picks

Takeaway/Casual

Chicken George Jnr

117 Tavistock Street, town centre

The Chicken George story started in Luton in 1985 when George Cheah opened his first fried-chicken counter, and his son Chris Cheah carried the name forward with Chicken George Jnr, opening the Bedford branch on Tavistock Street in August 2022. The national credentials are real: in 2016 the brand beat more than 35,000 rivals to win Best Takeaway in Britain at the Just Eat awards, and Chris has since teamed up with The Ribman of London to clean up at Wing Fest and Wing Jam, Europe's biggest wing competitions. The Bedford site is part sports bar, part restaurant, part takeaway, and the crispy wings in their layered hot sauce are what the town talks about. Updated February 2026 listings confirm trading.

Coffee with Art

82 High Street, town centre

Coffee with Art opened on 20 December 2013, the creation of friends Ian Tarvit and Michal Polak, in a building on Bedford High Street that dates to the 1870s - the second-oldest shop front on the street, formerly home to T. P. Clare and Sons watchmakers for nearly ninety years. The interior has its own personality: a wall of salvaged book spines, a staircase of books and an exploding-library feature give it the feel of a literary retreat rather than a coffee chain. The coffee is direct-trade, served as espresso and filter with rotating origins, and the cafe offers a full brunch and lunch menu with cakes baked on site. Local artwork lines the walls and changes every few months. Updated March 2026 listings confirm trading, Monday to Saturday 8.30am-6pm, Sunday 10am-5pm.

Il Basilico

12 Mill Street, town centre

Il Basilico is a small, family-run Italian restaurant on Mill Street, a short walk from the River Great Ouse, and it currently sits as the highest-rated independent restaurant in Bedford with a 4.9-star average across nearly 900 reviews. Chef Alex draws on ten years across busy kitchens in Norfolk, Bedfordshire and Hertfordshire and sources produce from local suppliers alongside specialist Italian imports to cook a menu that runs from freshly made pasta and wood-fired pizza to risottos and sharing antipasto. The tone is a restaurant that takes the cooking seriously without the formality: small room, attentive service, and dishes that make full use of Bedford's Italian community connection - the town has fed itself on this cuisine since the 1950s. Updated April 2026 listings confirm trading.

The Wellington Arms

40-42 Wellington Street, town centre

The Wellington Arms on Wellington Street is Bedford's most decorated real-ale pub, holding the CAMRA North Bedfordshire Town Pub of the Year 2025 title under landlady Liz Sutton and owner Burlison Inns - it is free of any brewery tie and independent in the truest sense. The bar runs eight handpumps serving rotating ales mostly from microbreweries, usually including a mild and a stout, with a further pair of pulls for draught cider, plus a well-stocked fridge of Belgian and international bottles. The walls are covered in pump-clips and breweriana built up over decades of passionate ale-keeping, and a small rear courtyard adds breathing room. The CAMRA listing was last updated 13 February 2026, confirming trading. Live music every Tuesday.

Bedford's Food Scene

Bedford's food identity is inseparable from its Italian community. In the early 1950s the London Brick Company recruited around 7,500 workers from the south of Italy - Apulia, Campania, Sicily, Abruzzo and Calabria - to fill post-war labour shortages in the Marston Vale brickworks. They settled along Midland Road, an area that became known as Little Italy, and within a generation had opened the specialist delis, cafes and restaurants that shaped the town's palate. By the late twentieth century roughly fifteen per cent of Bedford's population was of Italian descent, making it by far the most Italian town in Britain.

The practical result is a town centre unusually well stocked with family Italian restaurants, proper espresso bars and wood-fired pizza, well beyond what a market town of 110,000 would normally support. Mill Street, St Peter's Street and the streets between the High Street and the River Great Ouse hold the concentration of independents. The Embankment - Bedford's riverside promenade along the Great Ouse - draws visitors and locals alike to its cafes and pubs with views across the water, particularly in summer when the terrace trade picks up.

Beyond the Italian thread, Bedford has a genuinely plural food scene shaped by successive waves of migration: Caribbean and South Asian communities have added further independent restaurants to the mix, and a craft-beer culture centred on venues like the Wellington Arms has grown in the back streets away from the chain-heavy High Street. The real-ale scene is strong enough to have produced multiple CAMRA pub-of-the-year winners, and the town's independent cafes have built a loyal local following on the back of good coffee and an unhurried atmosphere.

Behind the Pass: The Kitchens That Keep Bedford Fed

Every kitchen in this guide, however small, runs an extraction system - canopy, baffle filters, plenum, ductwork and fan - that pulls heat, smoke and grease-laden air off the cookline. Bedford's Italian community brought the wood-fired pizza oven and the espresso machine to a market town that now runs a busy evening trade across its independent restaurants, and every high-output kitchen exhaust carries a concentrated grease load into its canopy.

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for a premises must assess and manage fire risk, and a grease-laden duct is one of the most serious risks in any catering building. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) reinforce the same duty of care, while an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) or a buildings insurer can ask to see documented proof the extraction system has been professionally cleaned.

What is TR19 Grease?

TR19 Grease is the UK industry standard for cleaning ductwork and extraction systems in commercial kitchens, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It defines how grease deposits are measured, the maximum thickness allowed before cleaning is due, and the post-clean certification a kitchen should hold - the benchmark insurers, fire assessors and EHOs look for.

A small kitchen is no less of a fire risk than a big one. A clean, certified extraction system is what keeps it safe and insured.

Industry Insight

The Cleaning Frequency Most Operators Get Wrong

Here is something many venue owners do not realise: under TR19 Grease, cleaning frequency is set by how many hours the kitchen actually cooks, not by how the canopy looks. A heavy-use kitchen running 12 to 16 hours a day should be cleaned roughly every three months. Moderate use of 6 to 12 hours points to every six months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours to every twelve.

The catch is that baffle filters can look clean while the plenum, the horizontal duct runs and the fan housing carry a heavy grease load that throttles airflow and lowers extract efficiency. A fire in that concealed ductwork spreads fast. That is why a dated TR19 certificate, kept in the fire logbook, is the document an insurer expects to see - and its absence at the point of a claim is a common reason cover is refused.

Planning Your Visit

The four picks sit within comfortable walking distance of one another in the town centre. Il Basilico on Mill Street and Coffee with Art on the High Street are a few minutes apart; the Wellington Arms on Wellington Street is a five-minute walk further west. Chicken George Jnr on Tavistock Street is a short walk north, back toward the railway station. Bedford Midland station connects to London St Pancras in under an hour and to the East Midlands via the Midland Main Line.

The Embankment riverside walk is worth adding to the day, particularly in good weather. Time it so that coffee at Coffee with Art starts the morning, a pasta lunch at Il Basilico follows, Chicken George Jnr handles the early-evening appetite, and the Wellington Arms closes the night with a range of ales that changes every visit. Book Il Basilico ahead - it fills quickly at weekends.

  • Start with a direct-trade flat white in the book-lined interior of Coffee with Art
  • Book Il Basilico ahead for lunch or dinner - it is the town's highest-rated table
  • Try the competition wings at Chicken George Jnr for a taste of the national-award standard
  • Ask the bar at the Wellington Arms which microbrewery mild or stout is on that week
  • Walk the Embankment along the River Great Ouse - Bedford's best riverside stretch

What to Order and How to Do the Day

Order with intent. At Chicken George Jnr, the wings in hot sauce are the national-award dish - order a plate and work through the heat levels. At Coffee with Art, ask which direct-trade origin is on the espresso that morning, and take a slice of whatever is baked that day. At Il Basilico, start with the mushroom bruschetta or calamari, then a bowl of freshly made pasta - the crab linguine has drawn consistent praise - or a wood-fired pizza from the open kitchen. At the Wellington Arms, let the handpump board guide you: the mild or stout from a Bedfordshire or East Midlands microbrewery is always worth asking about first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Bedford Food and Drink FAQ

Why does Bedford have so many Italian restaurants?

In the early 1950s the London Brick Company recruited around 7,500 workers from southern Italy to fill post-war labour shortages in the Marston Vale brickworks. They settled along Midland Road, which became known as Little Italy, and within a generation had established the cafes, delis and restaurants that still define Bedford's food scene. By the late twentieth century roughly fifteen per cent of the town was of Italian descent.

What is the best Italian restaurant in Bedford?

Il Basilico on Mill Street is currently Bedford's highest-rated independent restaurant, with a 4.9-star average across nearly 900 reviews. Chef Alex cooks freshly made pasta, wood-fired pizza and risotto using local produce and specialist Italian imports.

Where can I find good coffee in Bedford town centre?

Coffee with Art at 82 High Street is Bedford's most distinctive independent cafe, serving direct-trade espresso and filter in a Victorian shop front from the 1870s, decorated with a wall of books and rotating local artwork. Open Monday to Saturday from 8.30am.

Which Bedford pub has the best real ale?

The Wellington Arms at 40-42 Wellington Street, CAMRA North Bedfordshire Town Pub of the Year 2025, runs eight handpumps of rotating microbrewery ales - usually including a mild and a stout - plus draught cider and a wide range of Belgian bottles. It is free of any brewery tie.

Is Chicken George Jnr really a national award winner?

Yes. The Chicken George brand won Best Takeaway in Britain at the Just Eat awards in 2016, beating more than 35,000 other takeaways, and Chris Cheah has since won at Wing Fest and Wing Jam, Europe's biggest chicken-wing competitions. The Bedford branch opened on Tavistock Street in August 2022.

What is the Embankment in Bedford?

The Embankment is Bedford's riverside promenade along the River Great Ouse. It is the social heart of the town in summer, lined with cafes and pubs with terrace views across the water. The Victorian pub at its southern end, The Embankment Hotel, was built in 1891 and is a Bedford landmark.
