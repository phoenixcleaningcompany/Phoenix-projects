Where to Eat in Watford

Quick Answer

For a fast answer: Ocean Bells Coffee on the High Street for artisan brunch since 2013, Tarboush on Market Street for Lebanese mezze and a Good Food Award Gold Seal, Nana's on St Albans Road for authentic Lebanese dining and a shisha courtyard, and the Nascot Arms on Stamford Road for cask ales and the legendary Mr Ting Thai kitchen. Four independents, four neighbourhoods, one compact Hertfordshire town.

Watford does not have one food scene so much as several, layered street by street. This guide does something simple: it picks one genuine standout in each of four everyday categories and uses them as doorways into the wider town.

The Four Picks

Cafe

Ocean Bells Coffee

133 High Street, town centre

Ocean Bells opened on Watford High Street in 2013, inspired by London's then-nascent speciality coffee movement, and became the town's number-one rated coffee outlet. It is a family-run business, working through the full spectrum: rotating single-origin espresso and filter, matcha and turmeric lattes, and a daily changing brunch menu of avocado toast, homemade cakes and gluten-free and vegan options. In May 2024 the team doubled the space by knocking through into the unit next door, adding comfortable seating and a bright extension. Dog-friendly and dementia-friendly, it sits two minutes from Watford High Street station and anchors the independent stretch of the High Street.

Restaurant

Tarboush

57 Market Street, town centre

Tarboush has been part of Market Street since 2010, when a Lebanese family set out to bring the cafe culture of Beirut to Watford: mezze shared at the table, shisha in the outdoor courtyard and the unhurried welcome of a Middle Eastern living room. The name is borrowed from the traditional fez hats worn in those cafes. Fifteen years on, the restaurant spans three floors with individually themed rooms and a large patio, and has earned the 2025/26 Good Food Award Gold Seal, two years running. The kitchen excels at smoky mixed-grill platters, spiced cauliflower, hummus made fresh daily, and vegetarian tagine. It opens seven days, eleven until midnight, and is Watford's most-reviewed independent.

Known for
Authentic Lebanese mezze and grill, Good Food Award Gold Seal 2025/26
Good for
A relaxed group meal or occasion dinner with shisha garden
Takeaway/Casual

Nana's Restaurant and Lounge

129 St Albans Road, north of the town centre

On St Albans Road, a mile north of the High Street, Nana's has established itself as Watford's go-to destination for Lebanese home cooking with ceremony. The family-run kitchen produces halal mezze — houmous, vine leaves, freshly made falafel and coriander potatoes — alongside full charcoal-grilled mains, with takeaway available on all dishes. The well-regarded Lebanese dessert menu features knafeh and baklava. The centrepiece for eat-in is the Arabian courtyard, where shisha and mocktails are served under lanterns, and belly dancers perform on Friday and Saturday evenings. The Tripadvisor Travellers' Choice award and a consistent 4.7 rating underline the welcome it gives to both takeaway regulars and visitors making a purposeful trip.

Known for
Arabian courtyard dining, fresh halal Lebanese mezze and takeaway, Friday and Saturday belly dancing
Good for

Pub

The Nascot Arms

11 Stamford Road, Nascot Wood

The Nascot Arms sits at the heart of the Nascot Wood neighbourhood, one of Watford's most characterful residential pockets. The pub is listed on the CAMRA WhatPub database and runs a Cask Club with rotating guest ales including Hogs Back and Kirkstall, priced flat at three-fifty a pint on Monday evenings. What makes it singular is the arrangement in the back dining room: Mr Ting Authentic Thai Cuisine operates here as an independent kitchen franchise, with all drinks coming through the bar. The Thai menu — slow-cooked ribs, fragrant curries, rice dishes — is locally beloved and regularly requires a booking. Sports screens, quiz nights and a sheltered garden round out a pub that covers both a serious pint and a proper meal.

Watford's Food Scene

Watford's food map spreads outward from a compact centre that has traded since the 12th century, when the Abbot of St Albans was granted a royal market charter. The High Street and the covered Watford Market remain the commercial spine, with independent food businesses tucked into the shopping streets and the two-storey market building itself. The Parade — the pedestrianised main drag — holds the town's busiest restaurants and bars, while Market Street, running just behind it, is where the longest-standing independents have put down roots.

North of the centre, St Albans Road has developed its own independent restaurant strip: Lebanese, South Asian and Middle Eastern kitchens draw regulars from across the borough. The Nascot Wood neighbourhood, further up from the High Street, is a quieter residential quarter whose pub scene runs to proper cask ale rather than chain-bar lagers. Cassiobury, to the west, has a park-side cafe culture of its own, with the green open space of Cassiobury Park drawing families and cyclists at weekends.

Watford does not have one defining signature dish in the way that some industrial cities do, but its food character is genuinely pan-Hertfordshire-meets-north-London: strong in Lebanese and Middle Eastern independents, a growing speciality coffee scene, and a pub culture shaped by proximity to both London commuter flows and the Watford FC matchday crowd at Vicarage Road. The Flourish Craft Bakery, run by the O'Connell siblings and now operating a cafe alongside its wholesale operation, supplies sourdough to Michelin-starred restaurants and won Britain's best loaf in 2021 and again in 2025 — a signal of the quiet quality that runs through the town.

Behind the Pass: The Kitchens That Keep Watford Fed

Every venue in this guide runs on a commercial kitchen, and behind each cookline sits an extraction system - canopy, baffle filters, plenum, ductwork and roof fan - pulling heat, smoke and grease-laden vapour out of the building. Watford's kitchens run hard: Market Street's Lebanese grill, the Nascot Arms Thai wok and the town-centre cafe brigade keep canopies loaded with grease-laden vapour across every service, and a busy matchday around Vicarage Road adds a further spike of high-volume cooking.

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for a premises must assess and manage fire risk, and a grease-laden duct is one of the most serious risks in any catering building. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) reinforce the same duty of care, while an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) or a buildings insurer can ask to see documented proof the extraction system has been professionally cleaned.

What is TR19 Grease?

TR19 Grease is the UK industry standard for cleaning ductwork and extraction systems in commercial kitchens, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It defines how grease deposits are measured, the maximum thickness allowed before cleaning is due, and the post-clean certification a kitchen should hold - the benchmark insurers, fire assessors and EHOs look for.

The food gets the headlines. The extraction system is what keeps the kitchen open, insured and safe - quietly, behind the pass.

Industry Insight

The Cleaning Frequency Most Operators Get Wrong

Here is something many venue owners do not realise: under TR19 Grease, cleaning frequency is set by how many hours the kitchen actually cooks, not by how the canopy looks. A heavy-use kitchen running 12 to 16 hours a day should be cleaned roughly every three months. Moderate use of 6 to 12 hours points to every six months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours to every twelve.

The catch is that baffle filters can look clean while the plenum, the horizontal duct runs and the fan housing carry a heavy grease load that throttles airflow and lowers extract efficiency. A fire in that concealed ductwork spreads fast. That is why a dated TR19 certificate, kept in the fire logbook, is the document an insurer expects to see - and its absence at the point of a claim is a common reason cover is refused.

Planning Your Visit

The four picks split across three distinct parts of town. Ocean Bells and Tarboush are both within five minutes of Watford High Street station and Watford Junction, making them the natural start and end of a central itinerary. Nana's on St Albans Road is a ten-minute walk north or a short bus ride, and the Nascot Arms is roughly the same distance in the same direction through Nascot Wood — the two pair well for an evening that begins with a pint and a Thai plate and ends with mezze and shisha.

Watford Junction connects to London Euston in roughly 20 minutes and to Birmingham New Street direct, so the town is very accessible. Parking is plentiful around the Harlequin shopping centre near the High Street. Tarboush and Nana's take bookings and both benefit from one, especially on Friday and Saturday evenings; the Nascot Arms is walk-in for the pub but Mr Ting's Thai kitchen regularly fills up and is best booked ahead.

  • Start with brunch and artisan coffee at Ocean Bells on the High Street
  • Walk five minutes to Tarboush on Market Street for lunch mezze or dinner
  • Head up St Albans Road to Nana's for the Arabian courtyard experience
  • Book the Nascot Arms ahead if you want Mr Ting Thai food with your pint
  • Use Watford High Street or Watford Junction station - both are central

What to Order and How to Do the Day

Order with intent. At Ocean Bells, ask what single origin is on the espresso and pair it with an almond croissant or the avocado toast. At Tarboush, build a mezze around the hummus, smoky cauliflower and a mixed grill platter to share. At Nana's, the vine leaves and coriander potato mezze set the table before a charcoal-grilled main, and the knafeh for dessert is the right finish. At the Nascot Arms, a rotating guest cask ale and Mr Ting's slow-cooked ribs or a fragrant Thai green curry.

Frequently Asked Questions

Watford Food and Drink FAQ

What is the best independent cafe in Watford?

Ocean Bells Coffee at 133 High Street, open since 2013, is consistently rated the town's top coffee outlet. A family-run artisan cafe serving single-origin espresso, homemade cakes and an all-day brunch menu, it doubled its space in 2024 and is two minutes from Watford High Street station.

Where can I find the best Lebanese food in Watford?

Tarboush on Market Street (open since 2010, Good Food Award Gold Seal 2025/26) and Nana's on St Albans Road are the two top choices. Tarboush is known for its three-floor restaurant and Mediterranean garden; Nana's for its Arabian courtyard and Friday and Saturday belly dancing.

Which Watford pub serves the best real ale?

The Nascot Arms on Stamford Road in Nascot Wood is listed on the CAMRA WhatPub database and runs a Cask Club with rotating guest ales. It also hosts Mr Ting Authentic Thai Cuisine, so a pint of cask ale and a plate of Thai food in the same visit is entirely possible.

Is there a Watford restaurant with outdoor dining?

Both Tarboush on Market Street and Nana's on St Albans Road have garden or courtyard areas. Tarboush has a large all-weather patio; Nana's has an Arabian courtyard where shisha is served alongside the full food menu.

How do I get to Watford by train?

Watford Junction has direct trains to London Euston in around 20 minutes and to Birmingham New Street. Watford High Street station, served by the London Overground, is closer to Ocean Bells and Tarboush. Both stations are walkable to the town centre.

Does Watford have a good food market?

Watford Market has operated continuously since the 12th century. Today it occupies a two-storey covered building on the High Street, with around 45 independent units alongside a street market running up The Parade, featuring fresh produce, street food and local traders.
