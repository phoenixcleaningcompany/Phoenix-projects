Comparison · Eye & face protection

Glasses, goggles or shield: pick by hazard route.

Eye protection fails by mismatch more than by absence: glasses worn where dust needed a seal, a shield worn alone where the eye needed its own barrier. The three tiers exist because hazards reach the eye by different routes — sort the route and the tier picks itself.

The tiers

Route decides tier

Safety glasses (spectacle-style, tested under EN 166, typically to impact grade F — 45 m/s particles) stop things travelling at the eye in a straight line: chips, swarf, wire ends. They're light, fog least, and are rightly the site default. What they can't do is seal — dust, mists and splashes go round them.

Goggles add the seal. Ventilated goggles handle coarse dust; sealed or indirect-vent versions handle fine dust, liquid splash and the grinding tasks that shower from odd angles — typically at impact grade B (120 m/s). The cost is fogging and comfort, which is why goggles get taken off exactly when they shouldn't be; anti-fog coatings and proper venting are what make a goggle wearable enough to survive the shift.

Face shields protect the face — and the standard's quiet rule is that a shield is usually secondary protection: worn over glasses or goggles, not instead. Grinding, wire brushing, chemical decanting and forestry visors all follow the same logic: the shield takes the spray and the fragments; the sealed layer beneath guards the eye itself.

Questions

Eye protection FAQ

When should goggles be worn instead of safety glasses?

When the hazard travels around lenses rather than straight at them: fine dust, powders, liquid splash and mist, or grinding debris from odd angles. Glasses stop direct particles; only a sealed goggle stops what floats and splashes. The seal is the protection, so fit and anti-fog matter.

Is a face shield enough on its own?

Usually not — shields are secondary protection for the face, worn over safety glasses or goggles. Fragments and splash can bounce behind a shield's open edges, so the eye needs its own sealed or impact-rated layer beneath for most tasks that justify a shield at all.

What do F and B mean on eye protection?

EN 166 impact grades: F withstands 45 m/s particles (typical for spectacles), B withstands 120 m/s (typical for goggles and shields), A 190 m/s (shields for the harshest tasks). A 'T' suffix (FT, BT) means the grade holds at temperature extremes.
