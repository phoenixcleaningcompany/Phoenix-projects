Buying guide · Women's fit

Women's workwear: 'unisex' is doing a lot of lifting.

A third of many site and healthcare teams are women wearing kit patterned on men — sleeves rolled twice, gloves a size too big, hi-vis like a tabard on a fence post. That's not a comfort niggle: excess fabric snags, oversized gloves fumble, and PPE that doesn't fit doesn't protect as certified. The fix exists; you just have to specify it.

The problem

Why 'unisex' fails, mechanically

Unisex workwear is drafted on a male block: straighter hip, longer torso, broader shoulder, bigger hands. Put it on a typical female frame and the failures are physical, not aesthetic — trousers that gape or won't rise, excess sleeve and jacket fabric that catches on machinery and ladders, gloves whose empty fingertips wreck dexterity and get deliberately removed, and hi-vis that drapes so the retroreflective bands sit wrong. Safety kit is tested and certified on the assumption it fits; oversized isn't a style, it's a derating.

What a real women's range changes: repatterned rise and hip on trousers, shorter back length and adjusted shoulder on jackets, narrower lasts on safety boots, and gloves in genuine small sizes with proportionate finger lengths — not the same garment graded down. Questions

Women's workwear FAQ

Is women's workwear actually different or just smaller?

Properly made women's ranges are repatterned — different rise and hip on trousers, adjusted shoulder and back length on jackets, narrower boot lasts, proportionate glove fingers — not men's garments graded down. That drafting difference is what makes the kit sit, move and protect correctly.

Does badly fitting PPE really reduce protection?

Yes. Gloves with empty fingertips destroy dexterity and get removed; excess jacket and sleeve fabric snags on machinery and ladders; draped hi-vis mispositions its reflective bands; boots on the wrong last cause the blisters that put people in trainers. PPE is certified assuming correct fit — oversized quietly voids the assumption.

Do employers have to provide women's-fit PPE?

Employers must provide PPE that fits and suits each wearer — supplying men's sizing only and calling it unisex doesn't meet that duty where fit is compromised. Women's-cut options now exist across nearly every category, so availability is rarely a defensible objection.
