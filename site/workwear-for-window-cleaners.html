What window cleaners actually wear.

Water-fed poles turned window cleaning into a trade performed inside its own weather system: whatever the sky does, the pole rains back down the arms. The kit problem isn't the occasional soaking — it's being wet as a baseline, in January, while staying presentable on a customer's drive.

The job

Dressed for rain that comes from your own pole

The water-fed pole runs pure water down the brush, the pole and — inevitably — the arms of whoever holds it. The answer is breathable waterproofs worn as workwear, not emergency kit: a waterproof-and-breathable jacket or smock (working arms overhead in a boil-in-the-bag PVC top is its own misery), waterproof over-trousers or bibs, and sleeves that seal or shed at the cuff, where most of the water actually arrives. DWR maintenance is a fortnightly reality in this trade, not an annual one.

Winter turns hands into the limiting factor. Wet plus wind at ladder height strips heat faster than dry cold ever does — EN 511-rated thermal grip gloves (the standard tests for wet cold, not just cold) with spares drying in the van are what keep a January round finishable. Footwear follows the same logic: waterproof, warm and seriously grippy — wet ladder rungs, mossy patios and soaped forecourts are the trade's slip circuit.

Questions

Window cleaner FAQ

What do window cleaners wear in winter?

Breathable waterproof top and over-trousers as the working outfit, wicking base layers underneath, EN 511-rated thermal grip gloves (tested for wet cold — the trade's actual condition) with dry spares in the van, and waterproof high-grip footwear. Wet-plus-wind is the enemy; cotton anywhere in the system loses.

What gloves are best for water-fed pole work?

Thermal grip gloves rated under EN 511 — the standard that tests convective and contact cold and water penetration — in a dexterous enough build to handle poles and clips. Carry spares: once a glove wets through, its insulation is finished for the day.

Do window cleaners need hi-vis?

On domestic rounds, rarely. On forecourts, retail car parks, roadside frontages and commercial sites, Class 2 hi-vis is commonly required by the client or just plainly sensible — vehicles reverse at exactly the height of a kneeling window cleaner. Many rounds keep a vest in the van as standard.
