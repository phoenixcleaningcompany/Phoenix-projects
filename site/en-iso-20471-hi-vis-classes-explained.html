EN ISO 20471 explained.

The standard behind every compliant hi-vis garment sounds like a code number, but it comes down to a simple idea: enough bright fabric to be seen by day, enough reflective tape to be seen at night. Here's exactly what it measures, and how the three classes fall out of it.

What it measures

Two materials, doing two jobs

EN ISO 20471 is the international standard for high-visibility clothing for professional use. It sets performance for two materials that do different jobs at different times of day:

  • Fluorescent background fabric — the vivid yellow, orange-red or red. It reacts with daylight UV to appear brighter than any natural colour, giving daytime conspicuity.
  • Retroreflective material — the silver-grey tape. It bounces light (chiefly vehicle headlights) straight back toward its source, giving night-time visibility.

A garment must carry a minimum area of each, and both must meet minimum performance for colour, luminance and retroreflection. Miss either and the garment isn't compliant — which is why a bright top with too little tape, or lots of tape on a dull base, both fail.

The class table

Where the three classes come from

The class is simply how much of each material the garment carries. These are the minimum areas for the highest-performing garment in each class.

Why a vest usually can't be Class 3. A sleeveless waistcoat physically can't hold 0.80 m² of background. Class 3 is reached with sleeves, or by combining a compliant jacket with hi-vis trousers so the whole outfit carries the area — a key practical point covered in the Class 2 vs Class 3 guide.

Colours & certification

Yellow, orange or red — and a UKCA/CE mark

Three fluorescent background colours are permitted — yellow, orange-red and red — each within defined colour and luminance limits. Which you pick is often set by the sector or by contrast against the working background: rail and much highways work call for orange, while general industry leans yellow.

Because hi-vis is Personal Protective Equipment, compliant garments are certified and carry a UKCA (or CE) mark with the standard reference. The garment label also states the class and the care instructions — including the figure that trips a lot of buyers up.

The wash-cycle limit

Compliance has a shelf life

Both materials degrade. Fluorescent dye fades under UV and repeated washing; retroreflective tape cracks, delaminates and dulls with wear and laundering. Because of that, EN ISO 20471 garments are marked with a maximum number of wash cycles over which performance is guaranteed — often somewhere in the tens of washes, but it varies widely by fabric and finish.

The practical rule: a garment past its wash rating, visibly faded, or with cracked tape should be treated as non-compliant and replaced — regardless of how "yellow" it still looks. For a team, that means budgeting hi-vis as a consumable, not a one-off buy.

Questions

EN ISO 20471 FAQ

What does EN ISO 20471 measure?

Two things: the minimum area of fluorescent background fabric, which provides daytime conspicuity, and the minimum area of retroreflective material, which returns headlight beams at night. A garment's class depends on carrying enough of both, and the material must also meet minimum photometric and colour performance.

Can a vest be Class 3?

Rarely. Class 3 requires roughly 0.80 m² of background material, which a sleeveless waistcoat usually can't provide. Class 3 is normally achieved with a garment that has sleeves, or by combining a compliant jacket with hi-vis trousers so the whole outfit carries the required area.

What colours are allowed?

Fluorescent yellow, orange-red and red backgrounds are permitted, each with defined colour and luminance limits. The choice is often driven by the sector — rail and some highways work specify orange — and by contrast against the working background.

How many washes does hi-vis last?

It varies by garment and is stated on the label as a maximum number of wash cycles. Washing and UV gradually reduce both the fluorescence and the retroreflective performance, so beyond that figure — or once visibly faded or the tape is cracked — it should be treated as non-compliant and replaced.
