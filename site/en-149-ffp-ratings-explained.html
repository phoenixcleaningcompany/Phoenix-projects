EN 149: FFP1, FFP2, FFP3 — decoded.

Disposable dust masks carry the most misunderstood rating in PPE. The FFP number matters — but the seal against your face matters more, and a top-grade mask worn over stubble can protect worse than a lower grade fitted properly.

How it works

A filter is only as good as its seal

EN 149 covers filtering facepieces — masks where the whole facepiece is the filter. The grades measure two things together: how much the filter material stops, and how much leaks in around the edges. That's why the standard's real-world lesson is about fit: air takes the path of least resistance, and a gap at the nose or a bearded jawline turns any grade into decoration.

UK workplaces requiring tight-fitting RPE need face-fit testing for each wearer — and a clean-shaven seal line where the mask meets the face. That's HSE's position, not a manufacturer upsell.

The letters

NR, R and D — the marks after the grade

NR (non-reusable) means single shift — bin it after use; most site disposables are NR. R marks reusable facepieces that tolerate cleaning per the maker's instructions. D means the mask passed the dolomite clogging test — it breathes acceptably even as dust loads up, which matters for full-shift wear in heavy dust.

Valves ease exhalation on long wear but filter nothing on the way out — a consideration anywhere protecting others matters (food production lines, clinical settings). And frequency changes the format decision: someone in FFP3 conditions daily is usually better served by a reusable half-mask with replaceable filters — cheaper per week and easier to fit-test — which is the reusable side of the disposable vs reusable question.

Silica is the one to respect. Cutting, grinding or drilling concrete, stone, brick and mortar releases respirable crystalline silica — a cause of irreversible lung disease at exposures that don't look dramatic. That work is FFP3 territory as a minimum, alongside dust suppression and extraction. If a task involves regular silica exposure, the answer is an RPE programme, not a box of masks.

Questions

FFP FAQ

What is the difference between FFP2 and FFP3?

Filter efficiency and permitted leakage: FFP2 stops roughly 94% of test aerosol, FFP3 roughly 99% with a tighter leakage allowance. FFP3 is the disposable grade for silica dust, hardwood dust and welding fume; FFP2 covers general construction dusts like plaster.

Do dust masks work with beards?

Tight-fitting disposables don't seal over stubble or beards — leakage around the edge bypasses the filter entirely. Bearded workers needing respiratory protection should use loose-fitting powered hoods or shave the seal line; a fit test will fail otherwise.

What does NR mean on an FFP mask?

Non-reusable: the mask is certified for a single shift, then binned. R-marked facepieces tolerate reuse with cleaning per the manufacturer. The D mark separately shows the mask passed a clogging test and stays breathable through a dusty shift.

Is face-fit testing legally required?

Where tight-fitting RPE is used to control workplace exposure in the UK, yes — HSE expects each wearer to be face-fit tested on the specific mask model they use, and refitted if the model changes or their face changes significantly.
