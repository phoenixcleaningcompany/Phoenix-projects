Standard explainer · EN ISO 374

EN ISO 374: chemical gloves, letter by letter.

Chemical gloves carry the most information-dense marking in PPE: a pictogram, a type letter and a string of code letters that name the chemicals the glove actually resisted in testing. Read properly, the label answers the only question that matters — will this glove survive this substance long enough?

The system

Types, letters and time

EN ISO 374-1 grades chemical gloves by how many of its standard test chemicals they resist, and for how long. Type A: at least 6 chemicals for 30+ minutes each. Type B: at least 3 chemicals, 30+ minutes. Type C: at least 1 chemical, 10+ minutes — splash-brief protection only. The letters printed under the beaker pictogram identify which chemicals passed: each code letter (A–T) is a named substance — A is methanol, K is sodium hydroxide (caustic), J is n-heptane, and so on through the list.

Breakthrough time is the honest number: it's how long the chemical takes to permeate the glove at molecular level — invisible, and unrelated to visible damage. A glove rated 30 minutes against caustic is a 30-minute glove, however intact it looks. Degradation (the material swelling or hardening) is tested separately, and disposable nitrile against a named solvent may be a minutes glove: right for a splash-and-doff task, wrong for immersion.

In practice

How the pros actually choose

Name the chemical
The COSHH assessment names it; the glove maker's permeation chart answers it — never buy on 'chemical resistant' alone
Time the task
A 15-minute breakthrough is fine for a 5-minute task with disposal after — and dangerous for an hour's immersion
Doff and bin
Permeated disposables keep permeating — glove-off technique and single use are part of the protection

Questions

Chemical glove FAQ

What do the letters on chemical gloves mean?

Each letter under the beaker pictogram is a specific test chemical from the EN ISO 374-1 list — A is methanol, K is sodium hydroxide, J is n-heptane and so on. The letters shown are the chemicals that glove resisted for the required breakthrough time in testing; match them against the substances you actually handle.

What is the difference between Type A, B and C chemical gloves?

Test breadth and duration: Type A resisted at least six of the standard chemicals for 30+ minutes each, Type B at least three for 30+ minutes, and Type C at least one for 10+ minutes. Type C is short-splash protection; sustained chemical work wants Type A in the right polymer.

How long do chemical gloves protect for?

Until breakthrough — the tested time the chemical takes to permeate the material, which happens invisibly and regardless of visible condition. Track the time in contact, not the look of the glove, and treat the manufacturer's per-chemical permeation data as the real specification.
