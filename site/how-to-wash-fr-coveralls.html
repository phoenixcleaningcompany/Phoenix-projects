How to wash FR coveralls without ruining the flame resistance.

Flame-resistant clothing fails quietly. A softened, oil-soaked or over-washed coverall can look perfectly fine and no longer protect the person wearing it. The good news: the rules are simple, and they matter more than the machine. Here's how to wash FR kit so it stays compliant.

The short answer

You can wash FR — but the rules are non-negotiable

Flame resistance isn't like ordinary durability. It's a specific property — built into the fibre (inherent FR) or applied as a finish (treated FR) — and a handful of everyday laundry habits can strip it out without leaving any visible sign. Fabric softener and chlorine bleach are the big two, but hydrocarbon contamination and hard-water deposits do it too.

Get the wash right and an inherent-FR coverall protects for its whole working life. Get it wrong and you can turn a certified garment into ordinary — flammable — clothing in a single cycle.

The one that catches people out: fabric softener leaves a film on the fibres that can itself be flammable and can mask the flame resistance. Never use it — not in the wash, not in the rinse, and not as a dryer sheet.

Step by step

Washing FR coveralls, in order

Read the label

Note the maximum wash temperature, and for treated-FR garments the maximum number of wash cycles the flame resistance is rated for. Inherent FR has no wash limit; treated FR does.

Deal with contamination first

Remove oil, grease and fuel promptly — hydrocarbons are flammable and defeat FR no matter how good the fabric is. Heavily contaminated garments usually need professional / industrial laundering rather than a domestic machine.

Prepare the garment

Empty the pockets, close all zips and studs, and turn the coverall inside out to protect the outer surface and any reflective tape. Wash FR separately from oily non-FR workwear to avoid transferring residues.

Wash correctly

Wash below the stated maximum temperature with a normal detergent. No fabric softener, no chlorine bleach, no starch. In hard-water areas, rinse thoroughly to limit mineral build-up that can reduce FR over time.

Dry gently

Dry as the label directs and avoid excessive heat — don't over-dry. Correct drying protects both the fabric and any finish.

Inspect & repair

Check for damage, thinning and contamination. Repair only with FR thread and matching FR fabric — a non-FR patch is a hole in the protection. Retire the garment once the FR is compromised, or once a treated-FR garment reaches its wash-cycle limit.

At a glance

The do & don't

Do

  • Wash below the garment's stated maximum temperature
  • Turn inside out and fasten before washing
  • Remove oil, grease and solvent contamination promptly
  • Use a normal detergent and rinse thoroughly
  • Repair with FR thread and matching FR fabric only
  • Retire treated-FR garments at their wash-cycle limit

Don't

  • Use fabric softener — coats fibres, can hold flammable residue
  • Use chlorine bleach — degrades FR fibres and finishes
  • Wash FR with oil-contaminated non-FR workwear
  • Starch, or tumble/dry above the stated maximum
  • Patch with non-FR fabric or ordinary thread
  • Assume "looks fine" means "still protects" — track wash cycles

Why lifespan differs

Inherent vs treated — how long the protection lasts

Inherent FR is engineered into the fibre itself (e.g. aramid or modacrylic blends). It can't wash out, so with correct care it protects for the life of the garment. Treated (FR-finished) cotton gets its flame resistance from a chemical finish, which has a finite life — the maker rates it for a maximum number of washes, after which it should be retired even if it still looks and feels fine.

That single difference decides your replacement budget: inherent FR costs more up front but lasts; treated FR is cheaper but is a consumable on a wash count.

Questions

FR care FAQ

Why can't I use fabric softener on FR coveralls?

Fabric softener coats the fibres with a film that can be flammable and can mask or reduce the garment's flame resistance. It's one of the most common ways FR clothing is unknowingly compromised in the wash, so softener should never be used — including in the rinse cycle or as a dryer sheet.

Does washing wear out flame resistance?

It depends on the fabric. Inherent FR is built into the fibre and keeps its protection for the life of the garment. Treated (FR-finished) cotton has a chemical finish with a finite life, rated for a maximum number of washes on the label — after which it should be retired even if it looks fine.

Can I wash FR coveralls with my other workwear?

Avoid it. Washing FR garments with oil- or solvent-contaminated non-FR workwear can transfer flammable residues onto the FR fabric, undermining its protection. Wash FR separately, and deal with heavy hydrocarbon contamination before it goes in the machine.

Does hard water affect FR clothing?

It can. Hard water and some soap-based detergents leave mineral deposits on the fabric that build up over time and can reduce flame resistance. Use a suitable detergent, rinse thoroughly, and in hard-water areas consider measures to limit deposit build-up.
