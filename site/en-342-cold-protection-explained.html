EN 342: dressing for below minus five.

EN 342 is the standard nobody meets until they take a cold-store job — then it's the number on the suit that decides whether a −25 °C shift is routine or miserable. Here's what it certifies, and the sweat-management logic the label can't teach.

What it certifies

Insulation you can compare between garments

EN 342 covers clothing — usually suits, or jacket-and-trouser ensembles — for environments below −5 °C: cold stores, blast freezers, winter outdoor work in genuinely cold conditions. The label carries:

  • The insulation value (Icler) — measured thermal insulation of the garment as worn, moving. Higher = warmer, and because it's a standard test, two suits' values compare directly.
  • Air permeability class (1–3) — how windproof the shell is. Class 3 is the tightest; it matters wherever air moves, which includes the fan wall of a blast freezer.
  • Optional water penetration class — for cold that comes with wet.

Manufacturers publish tables translating insulation values into safe wear times by temperature and activity level — the practical planning tool for freezer rotations.

The craft

At minus twenty, sweat beats cold as the hazard

The counterintuitive rule of freezer work: over-dressing is as dangerous as under-dressing. Work hard in too much insulation, sweat into the layers, and the moisture kills the suit's warmth from inside — the second half of the shift is spent cold in wet kit. That's why EN 342 suits are sized to fit over a wicking base layer, not a pile of jumpers, and why door-transition roles (chamber to ambient and back) need layers they can vent.

The extremities aren't in the suit's rating: CI-marked insulated footwear (the boot codes), lined gloves and covered ears complete the system. The operational side — rotations, zones, who buys what — is in the warehouse sector guide.

Questions

EN 342 FAQ

What is EN 342 on a cold store suit?

The European standard for protective clothing against cold below −5 °C. The label's insulation value (measured as worn, moving) lets you compare garments directly, alongside an air-permeability (windproofing) class and an optional water-penetration class.

What's the difference between EN 342 and EN 14058?

Temperature scope: EN 342 covers genuinely cold environments below −5 °C — freezer and cold-store territory; EN 14058 covers cool environments above that, where lined bodywarmers and winter mid-layers do the job. Blast-freezer work is always EN 342 kit.

Why do I still get cold in a rated freezer suit?

Most often it's sweat: over-layering under the suit or over-working in it dampens the insulation, which then loses its warmth. Wear a wicking base layer, size the suit as designed, and vent during warm-side tasks. Wind-chill from evaporator fans is the other hidden factor.

How long can you work at −25 °C?

It depends on the garment's insulation value and your activity level — manufacturers publish wear-time tables matching insulation values to temperatures and work rates, and employers build rotation schedules from them. The suit's rating is the input, not the answer.
