What Does BESA Stand For and Why It Matters

BESA is the trade body behind TR19, the specification that decides whether your kitchen extract clean actually counts. Here is what the four letters mean and why your insurer cares.

Phoenix Duct Clean · 7 min read · Updated July 2026

You have paid for a duct clean, filed the certificate, and moved on with your day - then your insurer asks a single question you cannot answer: was it done to BESA standard?

It is a small phrase that carries a great deal of weight. Four letters on a compliance report, easy to skim past, yet they sit behind fire risk assessments, insurance renewals and the paperwork that keeps a commercial kitchen trading. If you run or maintain a busy extract system, sooner or later someone - an underwriter, a fire officer, an incoming landlord - will want to know that the work meets it. So it is worth understanding what those letters actually mean, who is behind them, and why they have become the reference point for cleaning the greasy ductwork above your cooking line.

What BESA is and where it came from

BESA stands for the Building Engineering Services Association. It is the trade body that represents the firms who design, install and maintain the mechanical services inside buildings - ventilation, heating, cooling, ductwork and the hygiene that keeps all of it working safely. When a specification, a training course or a competence scheme in this field carries the BESA name, it is coming from that association rather than from any one contractor selling a service.

A long history under a newer name

The organisation is far older than the acronym suggests. Its roots run back over a century, and from 1963 it was known as the Heating and Ventilating Contractors' Association, or HVCA. On 1 March 2012 it rebranded as the Building & Engineering Services Association, and in January 2016 it trimmed the name once more - dropping the ampersand - to become simply the Building Engineering Services Association. The change reflected how much broader the members' work had grown beyond heating and ventilating alone. If you come across older documents referencing HVCA guidance, you are looking at the same lineage that publishes today's BESA standards.

Why a trade body sets the rules

Ventilation hygiene is not tightly prescribed by a single piece of legislation. The law tells you a system must be kept clean and safe - the detail of how you prove that is where an industry standard fills the gap. BESA carries authority here for a few practical reasons:

  • It develops its specifications with input from cleaning contractors, insurers, fire engineers and manufacturers, so the guidance reflects real practice rather than one commercial viewpoint.
  • Its documents are the ones insurers and fire risk assessors most commonly name when they ask for evidence of a compliant clean.
  • It runs the training and competence schemes that decide who is qualified to carry out and sign off the work.
  • It maintains a register of vetted contractors, giving building owners a way to check a firm's credentials before hiring.

In short, BESA turns a vague legal duty into something measurable. That is precisely why the phrase matters on your paperwork - it signals that the work was judged against an agreed benchmark, not simply declared clean.

Why it matters for your kitchen extract

The BESA document you will hear about most is TR19. For years it covered internal cleanliness of ventilation systems as a whole, but in 2019 the association split out a standalone specification, TR19® Grease, dealing specifically with fire risk from grease building up inside kitchen extraction systems. Its companion, TR19® Air, covers general ventilation hygiene and indoor air quality. For anyone running an extract canopy over a cooking line, TR19 Grease is the one that governs your obligations - and it is where the four letters stop being trivia and start affecting whether you are covered.

The thresholds a compliant clean has to hit

What makes the standard useful is that it deals in numbers rather than opinions. Grease deposits are measured at points along the ductwork using a wet film thickness test or a deposit thickness test, and the readings drive the verdict:

  • Grease should be controlled so it does not exceed a mean average of 200 microns across the system between scheduled cleans.
  • Any localised deposit of 500 microns or more calls for immediate attention, as it represents a serious fuel load in the event of a fire.
  • After cleaning, the system should be brought back to below 50 microns on average, with before-and-after evidence recorded.
  • Recommended cleaning frequency is driven by daily usage - broadly, heavy use of twelve to sixteen hours a day points to roughly quarterly cleaning, moderate use of six to twelve hours to around every six months, and light use to about annually.

Those figures are a starting point, not a ceiling. TR19 Grease expects a competent person to set the actual interval through a cleanliness risk assessment that weighs your style of cooking, the hours you run and how much of the system can realistically be reached. A charcoal grill running all day is a very different proposition to a light breakfast service, and the standard is built to reflect that.

Insurance, competence and staying covered

The reason building owners care so much comes down to consequence. Grease is fuel, extract ductwork carries it right through the fabric of a building, and a hidden fire in a poorly maintained run spreads fast. Insurers know this, which is why many policies now expect cleaning to the BESA specification and proper records to back it up. Cleaning that misses the standard - or a certificate without measurement evidence behind it - can leave you exposed on a claim even though you paid for the work. There is a competence dimension too: TR19 Grease sets minimum standards for the technicians doing the job, underpinned by the Grease Hygiene Technician qualification, and BESA's Vent Hygiene Register lets you confirm a contractor is genuinely assessed against the scheme rather than simply claiming to follow it. When you commission a clean, it is worth asking who will sign it off and what evidence you will receive - a compliant job leaves you with photographs, access records and thickness readings, not just an invoice. That documentation is what turns a routine maintenance visit into something you can show a fire officer or an underwriter with confidence, and it is the same discipline that sits behind sensible cleaning schedules that stand up to inspection. The wider hygiene picture matters as well, since the same association's air specification ties into indoor air quality in commercial buildings - clean ductwork protects the people breathing the air, not only the building itself.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What does BESA stand for?

BESA stands for the Building Engineering Services Association. It is the UK trade body representing firms that design, install and maintain building services such as ventilation, ductwork and extract systems. It was formerly the Heating and Ventilating Contractors' Association, or HVCA, and adopted its current name in stages between 2012 and 2016. In the context of duct cleaning, BESA is the body that publishes the TR19 specifications your cleaning work is judged against.

Is a BESA-standard clean actually required by law?

The law requires you to keep extract systems clean and safe, but it does not spell out the exact method - that is where the BESA TR19 Grease specification comes in. It is the benchmark insurers, fire risk assessors and fire officers most commonly ask for as proof of a compliant clean. So while the standard itself is not a statute, meeting it is often the practical way to satisfy your legal duties and keep your insurance valid. A clean that ignores it can leave you exposed even if the work was carried out.
