The FSA vs the EHO: Who Does What

The FSA and the EHO are easy to muddle, but only one of them walks through your kitchen. Here is who sets the rules, who grades you, and how it reaches your extraction system.

Who does what

Two sets of initials get thrown around whenever food safety comes up - the FSA and the EHO - and they are not the same thing, nor do they do the same job.

If you run a commercial kitchen, it helps to know exactly who sets the rules and who turns up at your door to check you are following them. The short version is this: the Food Standards Agency (FSA) is the national body that writes the framework and runs the headline schemes, while the Environmental Health Officer (EHO) is the local council officer who actually walks through your kitchen and grades it. One is strategy; the other is the person with the clipboard.

Getting the distinction right matters because the paperwork, the powers and the point of contact all differ. You cannot ring the FSA to appeal a rating, and the EHO did not personally invent the standard they are holding you to. Below we set out what each does well, where each stops, and how it all lands on your extraction canopy and ductwork.

The FSA - the national rule-setter

The Food Standards Agency is a non-ministerial government department covering England, Wales and Northern Ireland. (Scotland has its own equivalent, Food Standards Scotland.) It exists to protect public health and consumer interests in relation to food. Crucially, it works at the level of policy and framework - it sets the standards, publishes the guidance and, in partnership with local authorities, owns the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme (FHRS). It does not, in the ordinary run of things, come and inspect your restaurant.

Where the FSA helps you

  • It defines the national framework, sitting on top of the Food Safety Act 1990 and retained EU Regulation 852/2004, so every business is judged against the same yardstick.
  • It owns and standardises the FHRS 0 to 5 rating, meaning a 5 in one town means the same as a 5 anywhere else in the scheme.
  • It publishes free, plain-English guidance - the Safer Food, Better Business pack among it - that tells you what “good” actually looks like before anyone visits.
  • It directly delivers controls in higher-risk sectors such as meat, primary dairy and wine production, taking that specialist burden off local teams.

Where the FSA stops

  • It will not inspect your premises, grade your kitchen or issue your certificate - that is not its job.
  • You cannot appeal a hygiene rating to the FSA; the appeal route runs back through the local authority that issued it.
  • It has no local enforcement powers over an individual business - it cannot serve you a notice or close you down.
  • Its guidance is national and general, so it will not tell you whether your specific extract system passes muster.

The EHO - the officer at your door

The Environmental Health Officer - increasingly called an Environmental Health Practitioner (EHP), the terms are interchangeable - is a qualified officer employed by your local council, not by the FSA. This is the person who carries out the actual food hygiene inspection, applies the FSA framework to what they see in front of them, and hands you the rating and the sticker shortly afterwards. They assess three things: hygiene standards, the structure and cleanliness of the premises, and how confident they are that food safety is being managed and documented.

Where the EHO helps you

  • They give you a real, specific verdict on your kitchen rather than general guidance - you learn exactly what needs fixing.
  • They are usually happy to advise on the spot, so a visit can be a free steer on how to move up the scale.
  • They are local and reachable, which means they - not a national helpline - are your route to discuss issues or lodge an appeal.
  • A strong inspection turns into a visible 5 rating and FHRS sticker that customers, insurers and landlords trust.

Where the EHO can bite

  • Visits are often unannounced, and the frequency scales with risk - roughly every six months for the highest-risk sites, up to two years for the lowest.
  • They hold real enforcement powers: improvement notices, hygiene prohibition and, in the worst cases, closing part or all of the business until it is safe.
  • Their “structure and cleanliness” assessment reaches into your extraction canopy, filters and ductwork - visible grease is an immediate red flag.
  • They can ask for documentary proof, and grease build-up with no cleaning records behind it will drag your score down.

Where it lands on your kitchen

Here is the join between the two. The FSA sets the standard that food premises must be kept clean and in good repair; the EHO decides, in your kitchen, whether yours are. When that officer looks up at your canopy, grease-caked filters, residue around the fan housing or drips from ductwork joints all count against the structure and cleanliness limb of your rating - and they read straight across to fire safety too.

That is why so many kitchens fail on the same point. Grease in the extract system is one of the biggest fire risks in any commercial kitchen, and up to 70% of commercial kitchen fires start in the extraction system. The recognised way to control it is the BESA standard now published as TR/19 Grease, which sets a post-clean target of no more than 50 micrometres of grease residue and produces before-and-after photos, grease-depth readings and a certificate. That certificate is exactly the documentary evidence an EHO can ask to see - and the same maintenance your insurer expects under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Is the EHO employed by the Food Standards Agency?

No. Environmental Health Officers are employed by your local council, not by the FSA. The FSA sets the national framework and owns schemes such as the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme, but it is the council's officer who carries out the inspection on the ground. So the person grading your kitchen and the body that wrote the rules are two different organisations.

Can I appeal my food hygiene rating to the FSA?

No. Although the FSA owns the rating scheme, appeals go back through the local authority whose officer issued the rating, not to the FSA directly. There is a set appeal window, usually 21 days, and a separate right to reply and a right to request a re-visit once you have made improvements. The FSA publishes the process, but the council administers it.

Does an EHO check the kitchen extraction and ductwork?

Yes, indirectly. One of the three areas an EHO assesses is the structure and cleanliness of the premises, and that includes your extraction canopy, filters and ductwork. Visible grease, residue around the fan housing or drips from duct joints all count against you and flag a fire risk. Keeping the system cleaned to TR/19 Grease standard, with a certificate to prove it, addresses exactly what the officer is looking for.
