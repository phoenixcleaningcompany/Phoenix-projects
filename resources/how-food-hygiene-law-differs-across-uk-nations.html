How Food Hygiene Law Differs Across the UK Nations

The food safety law underneath your kitchen is broadly shared across the UK, but the way your rating is scored and displayed changes the moment you cross a national border. Food hygiene across the UK

One kitchen, four rulebooks

If you operate kitchens in more than one part of the UK, it is tempting to assume the rules travel with you. They mostly do - until you reach the point that customers actually notice.

The core food safety law is broadly shared. Every commercial kitchen in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland has to run a documented food safety management system built on HACCP principles, and that requirement flows from the same piece of assimilated law across Great Britain. Where the four nations part company is in how your hygiene standard is scored, whether you are legally obliged to show it to the public, and what happens if you do not.

Those differences are not academic. A rating that stays quietly in a drawer in one nation must be stuck to the door in another, and the £200 penalty for getting it wrong is real. This guide walks through what changes at each border, so a business that trades in two or more nations knows exactly where the goalposts move.

Where the four nations diverge

Read these in order. The first point is the ground everyone stands on; the rest are the ways each nation builds something different on top of it.

  1. The shared legal foundation. Since 1 January 2024, Regulation (EC) 852/2004 has sat in UK law as assimilated law under the Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023, and its substance is unchanged. It means every food business, in every nation, must have a food safety management system based on the seven HACCP principles - hazard analysis, critical control points, monitoring, corrective action and records. That baseline is your starting point wherever you trade.
  2. England scores you 0 to 5, but display stays voluntary. Under the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme (FHRS), an officer rates you from 0 (urgent improvement necessary) to 5 (very good). England is the only nation where showing that rating to the public is encouraged rather than required, which is why only around 69 per cent of English businesses actually display a sticker. The Chartered Institute of Environmental Health continues to campaign for mandatory display here, so the position may not hold forever.
  3. Wales made display the law first, back in 2013. The Food Hygiene Rating (Wales) Act 2013 turned the 0 to 5 scheme into a statutory one and made display compulsory. Your sticker must sit in a prominent place - the front door, entrance or window - where a customer can read it before they decide to come in. Compliance shows in the numbers: roughly 92 per cent of Welsh businesses display, the highest in the UK.
  4. Northern Ireland followed in 2016 with the same 0 to 5 scale. The Food Hygiene Rating Act (Northern Ireland) 2016 replaced the earlier voluntary scheme and made display mandatory at or near every customer entrance. Around 91 per cent of businesses comply, and more recent rules have extended display duties to online ordering platforms so a customer sees your rating before they buy.
  5. Scotland runs a different scheme entirely. Rather than a 0 to 5 number, Scotland uses the Food Hygiene Information Scheme (FHIS), overseen by Food Standards Scotland, which awards a Pass or an Improvement Required outcome after inspection. All 32 Scottish local authorities take part. Display is encouraged rather than legally forced, and the underpinning duties sit in the Food Safety (Scotland) Regulations 2006 and the Food (Scotland) Act 2015.
  6. Getting the display wrong carries a penalty in Wales and Northern Ireland. In both nations, failing to display, showing an out-of-date sticker or misleading a customer about your rating is an offence. Enforcement typically runs through a £200 fixed penalty notice - reduced to £150 in Wales if paid within 14 days - and, in Northern Ireland, prosecution can bring a fine of up to £1,000 on conviction. Dozens of Welsh businesses have already taken £200 notices for not displaying.
  7. You keep the right to challenge and to reply. Across the schemes you have an appeal window - 21 days from your rating notification in Wales - decided by an officer who was not involved in the original assessment. You can also submit a right to reply that is published alongside your score on the regulator's website, and you can request a re-visit once you have put things right. Use these routes rather than leaving a poor rating on display for months.

Why the differences matter for your rating

The practical sting is not the letter of the law - it is visibility. In Wales and Northern Ireland, a middling score is not something you can quietly manage; it is fixed to your door for every customer to weigh up before they walk in. A business that would coast on a 3 in England is publicly holding that 3 at the entrance a few miles across the border. For multi-site operators, that turns hygiene into a brand-consistency problem as much as a compliance one.

It also matters where your marks come from. Every FHRS rating rests on three components - food handling and hygiene, the safety management procedures behind them, and the cleanliness and condition of the premises, including the structure, ventilation and equipment. That third area is where a lot of avoidable points are lost. Heavy grease loading in an extraction canopy, a neglected duct run or a filter that has not been touched in months all read as poor structural condition to an inspecting officer, and they drag the whole score down regardless of how tidy your food handling is on the day.

For anyone running kitchens across nations - a contract caterer, a hospitality group, or a busy university catering team feeding students across long days - the safest approach is to work to the strictest common denominator. Assume the rating will be displayed, assume it will be read, and keep the evidence that supports your cleanliness and condition score current: extraction cleaning certificates to the recognised standard, duct access records, and dated photographs of the ventilation system. That documentation is exactly what an officer wants to see, and it is the same evidence in all four nations even when the scheme name on the certificate changes.

None of this replaces the day-to-day discipline of good food handling, but it removes an entire category of easy losses. If your structure and ventilation are demonstrably clean and well maintained, you protect the one component of the rating that is hardest to fix in the hour before an inspection - and easiest to lose if you let it slide.

The short version to keep on file

Strip away the detail and the message is simple: the food safety law is largely shared, but display duties and scoring are devolved. Plan for the strictest of the four, keep your ventilation and structural evidence current, and you will not be caught out by a border.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I legally have to display my food hygiene rating?

It depends on the nation. In Wales and Northern Ireland display is a legal requirement, and you can be fined around £200 for failing to show your rating or for displaying an incorrect one. In England and Scotland display is encouraged but not legally compulsory, though if you do display a rating it must be accurate.

Why does Scotland not use a 0 to 5 food hygiene rating?

Scotland runs a separate scheme, the Food Hygiene Information Scheme, overseen by Food Standards Scotland rather than the Food Standards Agency. Instead of a 0 to 5 number it awards a Pass or an Improvement Required outcome after inspection. The underlying food safety duties are equivalent to the rest of the UK, but the public-facing result looks different.

If I run kitchens in more than one UK nation, how should I handle the different rules?

Work to the strictest common standard rather than the local minimum. Assume your rating will be displayed and read by customers, and keep the evidence behind your cleanliness and condition score current in every site - extraction cleaning certificates, duct access records and dated photographs. That same evidence supports your score whether the scheme is called FHRS or FHIS.
