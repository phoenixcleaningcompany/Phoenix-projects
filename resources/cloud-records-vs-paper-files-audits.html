Cloud Records vs Paper Files for Audits

Both cloud and paper are accepted by inspectors - what separates them is whether you can produce current, timestamped evidence the moment an auditor asks. Here is how each holds up when it counts.

Records & compliance

When the auditor asks, can you find it in ten seconds?

An audit rarely fails on the state of your ductwork - it fails on the paperwork you cannot lay your hands on quickly enough.

Whether it is an environmental health officer reviewing your due diligence, a fire risk assessor checking your kitchen extract, or an insurer testing a claim, the question is always the same: show me the evidence, and show me it is current. A drawer of dog-eared paper and a shared cloud folder can both answer that question. The difference is how reliably they do it under pressure, how well they survive a spilled coffee or a departed manager, and how much they help you rather than trip you up. This page weighs cloud records against paper files for the audits a commercial kitchen actually faces, from Safer Food, Better Business diaries to your TR19® Grease certificate.

Building a record system that survives an audit

You do not have to pick a single medium and live with it forever. The strongest setups borrow the discipline of paper and the resilience of the cloud. Work through these steps and you will end up with records an auditor trusts on sight, whichever way you store them.

  1. Map what you are actually required to keep. List every record an inspector might ask for: fridge and hot-holding temperatures, cleaning schedules, delivery and probe checks, pest control visits, staff training, and your kitchen extract cleaning certificate. The Food Standards Agency's Safer Food, Better Business pack sets the baseline for the food-safety side, and your insurer and fire risk assessment set the rest.
  2. Decide your retention period and stick to it. Many auditors expect to see three years of TR19® documentation, and cleaning intervals should never exceed twelve months for a busy kitchen. Agree a clear rule - keep daily diaries until the next local authority visit at minimum, and hold cleaning certificates and reports for at least three years - so nothing is binned early or hoarded needlessly.
  3. Insist on timestamps you cannot argue with. Back-filled records are one of the quickest ways to dent a Confidence in Management score. A digital entry logs the exact date, time and user automatically, which removes the suspicion that a week of temperatures was written up in one sitting the morning of the inspection.
  4. Store the certificate that carries real weight. Your post-clean verification report is the single document an insurer or fire officer will reach for first. It should name the qualified technician, state the date and location, include before-and-after photographs from matching positions, and record Deposit Thickness Test readings confirming grease is below the 200 micron threshold set out in TR19® Grease.
  5. Build in a backup you have actually tested. Paper burns, floods and fades; a single cloud copy can be deleted by accident. Whichever you lean on, keep a second copy somewhere independent - a printed certificate in the office plus the PDF in the cloud is a sensible belt-and-braces approach for the documents that matter most.
  6. Control who can see and change what. Good digital systems offer access controls and an audit trail showing who edited a record and when. That protects you from quiet tampering and gives an inspector confidence that the history in front of them is genuine rather than tidied up after the fact.
  7. Rehearse the retrieval. Once a quarter, pretend the officer has arrived unannounced and try to produce your last extract clean certificate, this week's temperatures and your pest log in under a minute. If you cannot, the problem is your filing, not your kitchen - fix it before someone official finds it for you.

Where cloud pulls ahead, and where paper still earns its place

The honest answer is that both are acceptable to enforcement bodies. The Food Standards Agency confirms that completed SFBB diary pages can be stored electronically or as printed copies, and digital SFBB+ packs are recognised by inspectors. Nobody is going to mark you down simply for using paper. What changes the outcome is reliability, and that is where the two diverge.

Paper is cheap, needs no login and never suffers a flat battery. In a wet, hot kitchen that counts for something, and a well-kept folder with dated, initialled pages remains perfectly defensible. Its weaknesses are equally plain: it goes missing, it degrades, it cannot be in two places at once, and when a manager leaves they often take the knowledge of where everything lives with them. If a fire officer asks for a certificate from eighteen months ago and it is in a box that got damp, the absence of paperwork is itself the finding - regardless of how clean the ductwork actually was.

Cloud records answer those weaknesses directly. Entries carry automatic timestamps, alerts chase the checks that have been missed, and everything is retrievable from a phone whether you are on site or not. For a group running more than one kitchen, central storage means head office can see compliance across every site without a car journey. The trade-offs are real too: you depend on a subscription and a connection, staff need to be comfortable tapping through a screen, and a system nobody updates is worse than a paper diary that gets filled in daily. The medium never cleans the extract or checks the fridge - people do.

For most operators the sensible destination is a hybrid. Keep the daily food-safety records digital so timestamps and reminders do the heavy lifting, and treat the high-stakes compliance documents - your TR19® Grease certificate, your fire risk assessment, your insurance schedule - as items you hold in the cloud and print, so a lost password never leaves you empty-handed in front of an insurer. That way the everyday discipline is automated and the documents that could void a claim exist in more than one place. Get these right, keep them somewhere you can reach in seconds, and the medium becomes a detail rather than a risk. Fall short on any one and the cleanest kitchen in the county can still fail on the paper.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Are paper records still acceptable for a food safety audit, or must I go digital?

Paper is perfectly acceptable. The Food Standards Agency confirms Safer Food, Better Business diary pages can be kept electronically or as printed copies, and no inspector will mark you down purely for using paper. The catch is reliability - paper goes missing, degrades and cannot prove when it was written, whereas digital entries carry automatic timestamps that protect your Confidence in Management score.

How long should I keep my TR19 kitchen extract cleaning certificate?

Aim to hold cleaning certificates and reports for at least three years, as that is the span many auditors and insurers expect to review. Bear in mind that cleaning intervals themselves should never exceed twelve months for a heavy-use kitchen, so you will usually have several certificates on file at any time. Keep them somewhere you can retrieve in seconds, because a certificate you cannot produce is treated as one you never had.

What actually makes a duct cleaning certificate stand up in an insurance claim?

The certificate must name the qualified technician, state the date and location, and include before-and-after photographs taken from matching positions. Crucially it should record Deposit Thickness Test readings confirming grease is below the 200 micron threshold set out in TR19 Grease. Without that evidence, an insurer can question whether the clean met standard, and a fire spreading through poorly maintained ductwork with no valid certificate can put your cover at risk.
