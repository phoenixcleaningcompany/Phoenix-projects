COSHH: The Acronym Every Employer Should Understand

COSHH stands for the Control of Substances Hazardous to Health, and behind those five letters sits a legal duty that reaches every commercial kitchen, workshop and back-of-house store cupboard. Here is what the regulations really ask of you - and where extraction and LEV testing come in.

COSHH in plain terms

COSHH stands for the Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002 - and it is one of the few pieces of health and safety law that touches almost every business in the country.

If your team handles cleaning chemicals, works around cooking fumes, breathes in flour dust, or relies on ventilation to keep the air clean, COSHH applies to you. It is not a document that lives in a filing cabinet. It is a set of duties you are expected to act on, day in and day out, and the regulator takes it seriously. The Health and Safety Executive estimates that around 11,000 deaths each year in Great Britain are linked to past exposure to hazardous substances at work, alongside roughly 22,000 new cases of work-related lung and breathing problems reported annually. Those numbers are the reason COSHH exists - and the reason understanding the acronym matters more than being able to spell it.

What COSHH asks of you, in three lists

The regulations can feel dense, but they boil down to a handful of clear obligations, a set of everyday substances you may not have flagged as hazardous, and a control chain that leads straight to your extraction and ventilation. Here is how it breaks down.

The core employer duties

  • Assess the risks - carry out a suitable and sufficient COSHH assessment before work with a hazardous substance begins.
  • Prevent exposure where you reasonably can, and adequately control it where prevention is not practicable.
  • Maintain, examine and test your control measures so they keep working as intended.
  • Monitor exposure and provide health surveillance where the risk calls for it.
  • Inform, instruct and train your people on the risks and the precautions.
  • Plan for emergencies such as spills, leaks or a ventilation failure.

Hazardous substances hiding in a commercial kitchen

  • Caustic degreasers and oven cleaners - strongly alkaline and capable of serious skin and eye burns.
  • Sanitisers and chlorine-based cleaners, which can release harmful vapours if mixed with acids.
  • Flour and grain dust, a respiratory sensitiser and a leading cause of occupational asthma in catering and bakery work.
  • Cooking fumes and airborne grease particulate carried through the extraction canopy and ductwork.
  • Combustion products such as carbon dioxide (CO₂) and other gases from cooking appliances.

The hierarchy of control

  • Eliminate the substance entirely where the job allows it.
  • Substitute it for something less harmful - a milder cleaner, a lower-hazard product.
  • Engineer the risk out with ventilation, enclosure and local exhaust ventilation (LEV).
  • Administer safe systems of work, procedures and training.
  • Reach for personal protective equipment (PPE) last, never first.

From acronym to action

The word that does the heavy lifting in COSHH is control. The regulations do not simply ask you to notice that a substance is hazardous - they ask you to prove you are keeping exposure below a level that protects health. For many airborne substances that level is set by a Workplace Exposure Limit, or WEL. These are the GB occupational exposure limits approved by the HSE and published in the guidance document EH40/2005, and the list is legally binding under COSHH. A WEL is a concentration of a substance in the air, averaged over a set period, and your controls need to keep exposure at or below it.

Some substances get stricter treatment still. Where a substance is classified as a carcinogen, a mutagen or an asthmagen - and flour dust sits in that sensitiser category - you cannot simply aim for the limit. You must reduce exposure to as low as is reasonably practicable, the principle often shortened to ALARP. That is a higher bar, and it is one reason good engineering controls matter so much. You cannot train your way out of a dust problem or rely on a mask to do a job that ventilation should be doing.

This is where a proper COSHH assessment earns its keep. It is not a tick-box exercise but a structured look at what you use, who is exposed, how badly and for how long, and what you are going to do about it. If you have never seen one done well, it is worth reading how COSHH assessments should actually be done - the difference between a generic template and an assessment grounded in your real processes is the difference between paperwork and protection.

The same logic applies to dust-heavy environments beyond the kitchen. In trades where fine particulate is the hazard, the engineering controls carry the whole burden of keeping people safe - and the consequences of getting it wrong are severe. The industry's hard lessons about silica dust and the health risks every site should know are a stark reminder that a substance you cannot easily see can still cause irreversible harm years down the line. Whether the dust is silica, wood or flour, the COSHH principle is identical: control it at source, and check that the control is working.

Why extraction and LEV sit at the heart of COSHH

When you follow the hierarchy of control down from elimination and substitution, you land on engineering controls - and in a commercial kitchen or workshop, that means your extraction system and any local exhaust ventilation. LEV is the equipment that captures contaminated air at or near the point where the hazard is generated, before it reaches anyone's lungs. A kitchen canopy drawing grease-laden vapour away from the cooking line is doing exactly this job, and so is a dedicated extraction arm over a dusty or fume-producing process.

Because LEV is a control measure, COSHH does not let you fit it and forget it. Regulation 9 requires that LEV systems undergo a thorough examination and test - often shortened to a TExT - at intervals not exceeding 14 months. That 14-month figure is a maximum, not a target: it exists to give a two-month scheduling buffer around an annual cycle, so a system tested in the same month each year stays comfortably compliant. Higher-risk processes demand far more frequent checks. Certain processes listed under Schedule 4 of COSHH, including wood dust and welding fume extraction, must be examined at least every six months, precisely because the substances involved carry known links to nasal cancer and serious respiratory disease.

A thorough examination is more than a quick look. A competent examiner - typically someone trained to the HSE guidance HSG258 and holding a recognised qualification such as the BOHS P601 - will measure airflow and capture velocities, check duct velocities, assess filter condition and fan performance, photograph the installation and review whether the system is genuinely controlling exposure to the level your assessment assumed. The resulting report tells you not just that the system runs, but that it protects. Records of these examinations must be kept for at least five years, and they are among the first things an inspector will ask to see.

Understanding COSHH, then, is really about joining the dots. The acronym names the law; the WELs and EH40 set the numbers; the hierarchy of control decides your approach; and LEV testing is the evidence that the engineering controls you have chosen are still doing their job. Miss any link in that chain and the protection quietly stops working - usually without anyone noticing until harm is already done.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What does COSHH stand for and who does it apply to?

COSHH stands for the Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002. It applies to almost every employer in Great Britain whose staff work with, or could be exposed to, hazardous substances such as cleaning chemicals, dusts, fumes, vapours or biological agents. In a commercial kitchen that covers everything from caustic degreasers to flour dust and cooking fumes.

What is a Workplace Exposure Limit (WEL)?

A WEL is a legally binding limit on the concentration of a hazardous substance allowed in workplace air, averaged over a set period. WELs are the GB occupational exposure limits approved by the HSE and published in the guidance document EH40/2005. Your control measures must keep exposure at or below the relevant WEL, and for carcinogens, mutagens and asthmagens you must reduce it to as low as is reasonably practicable.

How often does LEV need to be tested under COSHH?

Regulation 9 of COSHH requires local exhaust ventilation to have a thorough examination and test at intervals not exceeding 14 months. The 14-month figure is a maximum that allows a small scheduling buffer around an annual cycle. Higher-risk processes listed in Schedule 4, such as wood dust and welding fume extraction, must be examined at least every six months.

Is a kitchen extraction canopy classed as an LEV control measure?

Yes. Any system designed to capture and remove contaminated air at or near its source is a local exhaust ventilation control under COSHH, and a kitchen canopy drawing grease-laden vapour away from the cooking line does exactly that. Because it is a control measure, it must be maintained, examined and tested so that it keeps controlling exposure. Records of examinations should be kept for at least five years.
