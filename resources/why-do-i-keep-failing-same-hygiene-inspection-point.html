Why Do I Keep Failing the Same Hygiene Inspection Point?

When the same line keeps coming back on your inspection report, it is not bad luck - it is a fix that treated the symptom and left the cause untouched. Here is how officers score repeat failures, and how to stop them.

If the same line keeps reappearing on your inspection report, it is rarely bad luck - it is usually a fix that treated the symptom and quietly left the cause in place.

Environmental health officers do not score in a vacuum. Every visit is logged, and when an officer opens your file they can see what was raised last time and whether it has genuinely gone away. So when a contravention comes back a second or third time, it stops reading as a one-off and starts reading as a pattern - and a pattern is exactly the thing that drags a rating down and keeps it there. This piece walks through what the officer is really recording, why the same point keeps failing, and how you break the loop rather than plastering over it before every visit.

How inspections are scored

The three things every officer is really scoring

Under the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme, an officer builds your rating from three separate areas, and they carry equal weight. The first is food hygiene and safety procedures - how you handle, cook, cool and store food day to day. The second is the structural condition of the premises - cleanliness, layout, lighting, ventilation and pest proofing. The third, and the one most operators underestimate, is confidence in management: your track record, your attitude, your knowledge, and whether your written systems actually match what happens on the floor.

That third area is where repeat failures do their real damage. Food hygiene and structural elements each score in bands of 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25, where a lower number is better. Confidence in management runs 0, 5, 10, 20, then jumps to 30 - so a single step in the wrong direction costs you a large block of points at once. When a contravention returns, the officer is not just re-scoring the dirty extractor or the warm fridge. They are asking a bigger question: can this business be trusted to keep on top of things without me standing here? Every time the same fault reappears, the honest answer moves further from yes, and the confidence-in-management score climbs. That is why fixing the visible problem the night before a visit almost never lifts your rating - the officer is scoring the pattern, not the panic.

Why the same point keeps coming back

Almost every repeat failure comes down to the same root cause: the last fix was an event, not a routine. Someone scrubbed the thing, the officer left happy, and the schedule that would have kept it clean was never written down or never followed. A deep clean that only happens when an inspection is looming is, by definition, a reactive one - and reactive cleaning is exactly what officers are trained to spot. They can tell the difference between a surface that is maintained and one that has been rushed the night before, because the two age differently.

The classic examples all share this shape. Temperature control is a common repeat point: a fridge creeps above 5°C, or hot holding drops below 63°C, and food spends too long in the 5°C to 63°C danger zone. You correct it on the day, but without probe checks logged morning and evening, the same drift returns before the next visit and the same note goes back in the file. The answer is not a better excuse - it is a records habit, and it helps to keep your probes and thermometers inspection-ready so the evidence is already sitting there when the officer asks for it. The same logic applies to cleaning schedules, staff training records and pest proofing. If the officer cannot see a documented system that names the task, the method, the product and the frequency, they have no reason to believe the fault will not return - and their scepticism is written straight into your confidence-in-management score.

There is a hard edge to this too. When a contravention is serious or persistent, the officer can issue a hygiene improvement notice that sets out exactly what is wrong, what you must do and a deadline of at least 14 days. They will come back to check. Miss that, and you are into prohibition and prosecution territory. The notice is not the punishment - it is the last warning that a repeat pattern has been formally noticed.

The point that quietly repeats: the parts you cannot see

There is one repeat failure that catches out even well-run kitchens, and it is the one hiding above your heads. Grease. You wipe the canopy, you send the filters through the dishwasher, and the visible surfaces gleam - but the extract ductwork running up through the ceiling void and out to the fan is never touched. Grease keeps building there, invisible and highly flammable, and it shows up under both the structural score and, increasingly, the fire-safety conversation an officer will have with you. Clean the parts you can reach and leave the parts you cannot, and you have not solved the point at all - you have simply moved it somewhere you will not notice until it fails again.

This is where a recognised standard matters. TR19 Grease, the BESA specification that superseded the old Section 7 of TR19, is the benchmark for managing grease in kitchen extraction. It sets clear numbers: where the mean grease deposit across the system exceeds 200 microns the full system needs cleaning, and a single reading above 500 microns demands immediate localised attention. After a proper clean, verification testing should confirm deposits below 50 microns, measured and recorded so you have proof. That proof does double duty - it satisfies the officer that your structure is genuinely maintained, and it protects your buildings and contents insurance, which many insurers will treat as invalid if you cannot show that extract ductwork has been cleaned to a recognised schedule. High-level and concealed cleaning is not an optional extra bolted on to the visible wipe-down. For a lot of kitchens it is the actual repeat point, dressed up as a structural note.

Breaking the loop, then, is less about working harder before each inspection and more about turning every recurring fault into a routine with a record behind it. Written schedules, logged temperatures, evidenced training, and a deep clean that reaches the ductwork and canopy on a planned cycle - do that, and the officer stops seeing a pattern of failure and starts seeing a business in control. That shift is what lifts the confidence-in-management score, and it is exactly the ground you need to be on if you want to pass a food hygiene inspection first time rather than fighting the same line year after year.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Does fixing the problem before the inspection improve my rating?

Rarely, on its own. Officers log every visit, so they can see the same fault has appeared before and been corrected reactively. A last-minute clean fixes the surface but not the pattern, and it is the pattern that shapes your confidence-in-management score. What lifts the rating is a documented routine - a written schedule, logged checks and evidenced training - that shows the fault is genuinely under control between visits.

Why does the same point count against me more each time it recurs?

Because a repeat contravention answers a bigger question than the fault itself. The officer is judging whether your business can maintain standards without supervision, and each recurrence makes the honest answer weaker. That judgement feeds the confidence-in-management area of your score, which moves in large blocks, so a returning fault can cost you a whole band at once even if the immediate issue looks minor.

Could grease in my extract ductwork be a hidden repeat failure?

Very often, yes. Kitchens clean the visible canopy and filters but leave the concealed ductwork building up flammable grease, which shows up under the structural score and the fire-safety conversation. TR19 Grease sets clear thresholds - a full clean once mean deposits exceed 200 microns - and cleaning to that standard with verification records both satisfies the officer and protects your insurance.
