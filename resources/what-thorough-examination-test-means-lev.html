What Thorough Examination and Test Means for LEV

Thorough examination and test - TExT - is the formal, COSHH-required health check that proves your kitchen extraction still controls fumes at source. Here is what it really involves.

LEV testing explained

Thorough examination and test - usually shortened to TExT - is the formal health check your local exhaust ventilation must pass to prove it is still controlling the fumes, grease and airborne contaminants it was installed to capture.

The phrase comes straight from the Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations 2002. Regulation 9 says that where you rely on engineering controls such as extraction to protect people, those controls must be examined and tested thoroughly at suitable intervals - and the guidance that fleshes this out is the Health & Safety Executive's HSG258, Controlling airborne contaminants at work. It is easy to read "thorough examination and test" as a quick airflow reading and a certificate. It is not. Done properly, a TExT is a structured investigation into whether your system still does its job under real working conditions, and it produces a written verdict you can defend to an inspector, an insurer or a poorly employee's solicitor.

For a commercial kitchen this matters more than most people assume. Cooking fumes are a recognised health hazard, and the HSE's own catering guidance is explicit that the extraction system should be examined and tested at least once every 14 months. Below is what that examination actually looks like, step by step, and what each part is really telling you.

What a thorough examination and test involves, step by step

HSG258 breaks a TExT into three connected parts - a thorough examination, a measurement of technical performance, and an assessment of whether exposure is actually being controlled. In practice a competent examiner works through the following.

  1. Reviewing the design intent first. Before touching a single duct, the examiner establishes what "good" looks like for your system. That means reading the commissioning report, the user manual and the logbook that should have been supplied when the system was installed, so there is a benchmark - original airflows, capture velocities and fan settings - to test today's readings against.
  2. Examining the whole system thoroughly. A visual and physical examination runs the full length of the installation, from canopy and grease filters through ductwork to the fan and discharge point. The examiner is looking for damage, corrosion, blocked or missing filters, disconnected sections, grease build-up, worn fan belts and anything that would stop the system working as intended.
  3. Measuring capture at the hood. The point of extraction is to catch contaminant at source, so the examiner measures face and capture velocities across the canopy. For a kitchen canopy this confirms that fumes rising off the cooking line are drawn in rather than spilling into the room and the staff working there.
  4. Measuring the technical performance inside the system. Duct velocities, static pressures and fan performance are recorded at set points and compared against the commissioning figures and manufacturer data. A drop against the benchmark is often the first hard evidence that grease is narrowing the ducts or that the fan is tiring, long before anyone notices a problem in the kitchen.
  5. Testing control qualitatively. Numbers alone do not prove containment, so the examiner adds qualitative checks - typically smoke or dust-lamp techniques - to watch how air actually moves and whether contaminant is genuinely captured and carried away during representative activity rather than escaping back towards people.
  6. Judging whether exposure is adequately controlled. The examiner pulls the evidence together into a single professional judgement: is the LEV, as found and in normal use, keeping exposure to a safe level? This is the assessment that connects the hardware back to your COSHH risk assessment and the exposure it was meant to control.
  7. Issuing a clear, written report with actions. You receive a report that records what was examined, the readings taken, photographs, hood labels or performance indicators, a pass or fail against each function, and specific remedial actions with timescales. A credible report tells you not just that something failed, but what to do and by when.

Why the detail matters - and what a good report gives you

The reason HSG258 insists on all three parts is that each catches a failure the others miss. A canopy can pull a healthy face velocity while a grease-choked duct quietly strangles the system downstream; a fan can spin happily while contaminant spills sideways out of a badly positioned hood. Only by examining condition, measuring performance and testing control together can an examiner say, with evidence, that your kitchen extraction is still protecting people. That is the difference between a certificate and a genuine thorough examination and test.

Competence is central to the whole exercise. The regulations require the work to be done by a competent person, and the recognised route in the UK is BOHS training - the P601 qualification for LEV examination and testing. An examiner working to that standard knows how to interpret readings against commissioning data rather than simply confirming that air is moving. When you commission a TExT, it is reasonable to ask what qualification the examiner holds and to see it named on the report.

The output is a document, not just a visit, and it carries duties of its own. You must keep the report and make it available, and records should be retained for at least five years. It sits alongside your wider paperwork - the extraction TExT is one of the things an inspector expects to find, so it is worth understanding the documents an HSE inspector will ask for and keeping them together. The examination also feeds back into your risk paperwork, because the "adequately controlled" verdict only means anything against the exposures you identified when your COSHH assessment was actually done.

On timing, treat the 14-month figure carefully. It is a legal maximum for most systems, not a target, and HSG258 sets shorter intervals for certain higher-risk processes - some as frequent as monthly or six-monthly. For a busy kitchen, the practical driver is grease: heavy use loads filters and ducts quickly, so many operators examine and clean on a tighter cycle than the statutory ceiling. A TExT that comes back with a clean bill and a sensible interval is also your evidence that the routine cleaning and maintenance in between are keeping pace.

If your last report is more than a year old or you have never seen the commissioning figures, book a thorough examination and test and get a clear benchmark on record.

The essentials to remember

Thorough examination and test is not a formality bolted onto your cleaning contract. It is the regulated proof that your extraction still controls the fumes it was built to catch, and it only holds up when it is done thoroughly, by a competent person, and written down clearly.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

How often does kitchen extraction need a thorough examination and test?

For most LEV systems the legal maximum interval is 14 months, set by COSHH Regulation 9, and the HSE's catering guidance applies this to cooking-fume extraction. It is a ceiling, not a target - HSG258 requires shorter intervals for some higher-risk processes, and heavy kitchen use often justifies more frequent examination and cleaning. If in doubt, treat 14 months as the latest you should go, not the norm.

What is the difference between an LEV test and a full thorough examination and test?

A quick airflow reading only tells you air is moving. A thorough examination and test has three parts - a physical examination of the whole system, measurement of its technical performance against commissioning data, and an assessment of whether exposure is genuinely being controlled. Only the full TExT produces the written verdict that satisfies COSHH and stands up to an inspector.

Who is allowed to carry out a TExT?

The work must be done by a competent person, and the recognised UK route is BOHS training such as the P601 qualification for LEV examination and testing. A competent examiner interprets your readings against the original commissioning figures rather than simply confirming airflow exists. It is reasonable to ask to see the examiner's qualification named on the report.
