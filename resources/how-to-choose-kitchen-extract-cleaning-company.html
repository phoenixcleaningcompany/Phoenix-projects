How to Choose a Kitchen Extract Cleaning Company

The cheapest quote and a glossy certificate are not the same as a compliant clean. Here is how to choose a kitchen extract cleaning company that actually protects your kitchen, your cover and your people.

A cautionary tale

The certificate was framed on the office wall, dated four months earlier, and it did not save them.

Picture a busy site kitchen running two shifts a day. The kitchen manager had done the responsible thing, or so it seemed. A contractor had been in, quoted keenly, cleaned the canopy and the visible run of duct, handed over a smart-looking certificate and left. Everyone moved on. Then a fan bearing overheated late one evening, a smoulder in the ductwork caught, and a small fire ran further up the extract than anyone expected.

The building was fine in the end. The insurance claim was not. When the loss adjuster asked for evidence that the whole extract system had been cleaned to standard, the paperwork fell apart. The certificate covered the canopy and the first few metres of accessible duct. The long horizontal run above the ceiling, where the grease had actually built up, had never been opened. There were no access panels to open it with, no before-and-after photographs, no grease-depth readings, and no schematic showing what had and had not been reached. The clean looked done. It was not.

This is the trap that a poor choice of contractor sets for you, and it is quietly common. Choosing a kitchen extract cleaning company is not about finding someone to make the canopy shine. It is about buying evidence that stands up when something goes wrong. Get that decision right and the rest follows.

The post-mortem

What actually went wrong

Nothing in that story was bad luck. Every failure traced back to how the contractor was chosen, and each one is avoidable.

The cleaning body of record in the UK is BESA, the Building Engineering Services Association, and its specification for this work is TR19® Grease. That document sets out how a grease extract system should be inspected, cleaned and verified. The kitchen above was measured against it after the fire and missed on almost every count. Here is where it came apart.

  • They bought a price, not a scope. The quote was cheap because it quietly excluded the hard part. Accessible surfaces were cleaned; the concealed horizontal duct, the very place grease travels and settles, was left. A clean that does not reach the whole system is not a compliant clean, it is a tidy-up.
  • There were no access panels. TR19® Grease expects access panels along the ductwork at regular intervals, broadly every 2 to 3 metres wherever the run allows, so that every internal surface can be reached, inspected and cleaned. Without them, large stretches are sealed off by design and no honest clean is possible.
  • Nobody measured anything. Compliance is a number, not an opinion. The standard works to a mean grease deposit that should not exceed 200 microns across the system, verified by deposit thickness testing (DTT) or wet film thickness testing (WFTT). No readings were taken, so there was no proof the grease had gone.
  • The paperwork was a certificate, not a report. A genuine Post-Clean Verification Report states plainly whether the system was cleaned in its entirety, includes before-and-after photographs, records the test readings, and carries a schematic marking access points and any areas that could not be reached, with recommendations. A one-page certificate with a date on it proves almost nothing.
  • Qualifications were never checked. The technicians held no relevant BESA training. The Grease Hygiene Technician (GHT) qualification exists precisely so the person cleaning and signing off the work understands the standard, the safety and the verification. Nobody had asked.

Put together, these were not five separate problems. They were one decision, made on headline price alone, playing out five different ways.

The good news is that a strong contractor is not hard to spot once you know what to ask for. Frequency is a useful starting anchor: heavy-use kitchens running roughly 12 to 16 hours a day are generally cleaned every 3 months, moderate use of 6 to 12 hours every 6 months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours every 12 months. Your insurer may set a shorter interval, and a good company will read your policy rather than guess. Work through the checklist below before you sign anything.

  1. Ask how they measure compliance, and listen for numbers. A serious contractor talks in microns, DTT and WFTT, and offers to show you the readings. If the answer is vague or all about how clean the canopy will look, keep looking.
  2. Confirm the technicians are qualified. Ask specifically whether the people on site hold the BESA Grease Hygiene Technician (GHT) qualification, and whether the same firm does the work or subcontracts it out. You want the qualified people actually on your roof, not a name on a form.
  3. Insist on a full Post-Clean Verification Report, and ask to see a sample. It should include before-and-after photographs, grease-depth readings, a system schematic, and a clear statement of whether the whole system was cleaned. If any part could not be reached, the report should say so and recommend what to do about it.
  4. Check the scope covers the whole system. Canopy, filters, plenum, fan and the full length of ductwork to the point of discharge. Ask them to walk the run with you and point out where the access is, or where new panels will be needed.
  5. Get access panels quoted in, not around. If your ductwork lacks panels at sensible intervals, a good company will price installing them so the system can be cleaned properly next time. A contractor who cleans only what they can reach and stays quiet about the rest is the one to avoid.
  6. Match the plan to how you actually cook. A steady daytime canteen and an intense, stop-start operation such as stadium and event catering that fires up hard around fixtures carry very different grease loads. The right frequency follows your real hours and menu, not a generic sticker.
  7. Check they carry the right insurance and references. Public liability, method statements and risk assessments for working at height and in confined spaces, and a couple of similar sites you can call. Reputable firms hand these over without being chased.

Why the paperwork is the point

It helps to remember why any of this matters beyond the visit itself. Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the responsible person for the premises has a legal duty to manage fire risk, and a grease-laden extract system is a well-understood fire route. When an insurer assesses cover, or a loss adjuster examines a claim, they do not stand in your kitchen and admire the canopy. They read the report. A Post-Clean Verification Report with photographs, readings and an honest schematic is the thing that turns a clean into proof, and proof into a claim that pays. That is what you are really buying, and it is why the cheapest quote so often turns out to be the most expensive.

So when you choose a kitchen extract cleaning company, choose the one that leads with evidence. The firm that measures, documents and tells you plainly what it could and could not reach is protecting you, even when the news is a little inconvenient. The firm that only ever tells you the job is done is protecting itself.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What qualification should a kitchen extract cleaning technician hold?

Look for the BESA Grease Hygiene Technician (GHT) qualification, which shows the person cleaning and signing off your system has been trained in the TR19 Grease standard, the safety procedures and the verification methods. Also ask whether the company does the work itself or subcontracts it, because you want the qualified people actually on site. A firm that cannot tell you who is doing the work, or what they are trained to, is a firm to avoid.

How do I know the clean was done properly and not just the visible parts?

Ask for a full Post-Clean Verification Report rather than a one-page certificate. It should include before-and-after photographs, grease-depth readings taken by deposit thickness testing (DTT) or wet film thickness testing (WFTT), and a schematic that marks the access points and any sections that could not be reached, with recommendations. If the report states clearly that the whole system was cleaned and backs it with evidence, you have proof; if it is vague about scope, treat that as a warning.

How often does a commercial kitchen extract system need cleaning?

Frequency depends on how hard the kitchen works. As a general guide under TR19 Grease, heavy use of around 12 to 16 hours a day is cleaned every 3 months, moderate use of 6 to 12 hours every 6 months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours every 12 months. Your insurance policy may require a shorter interval, so a good contractor will check your cover rather than assume, and set the schedule to your real cooking hours and menu.
