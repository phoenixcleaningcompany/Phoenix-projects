Kitchen extract at scale

A food court can pack a dozen or more cooking concessions under one roof, yet share a single building, a single fire strategy and, very often, tangled runs of extract ductwork - so the question is not whether the extract system gets cleaned, but who owns that duty and how it is coordinated.

Compliance in a busy food court is a scale problem before it is a cleaning problem. Each unit produces its own grease-laden air, each has its own canopy and filters, and the ducts frequently merge in ceiling voids and risers that no single tenant can reach. Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 there is rarely one Responsible Person here - the centre operator, the managing agent and every catering tenant can each hold part of the duty, which is exactly why coordination matters so much.

The industry benchmark is BESA's TR19® Grease, which sets out how kitchen extract systems should be inspected, measured and cleaned to control fire risk. It uses a wet film thickness test to gauge residual grease in microns, and treats a mean deposit approaching 50 microns as the trigger for cleaning. The recognised frequencies follow cooking intensity - roughly every three months for heavy use such as continuous frying or chargrilling, every six months for moderate use, and annually for light use. In a food court those categories sit side by side, which forces a genuine choice about how you organise the work.

Below we weigh the two models operators actually use - letting each tenant arrange its own cleaning, versus running one centre-wide programme - so you can see the trade-offs plainly.

Option A: unit-by-unit cleaning arranged by each tenant

In this model every concession is left to book, evidence and pay for its own extract clean. It mirrors how a standalone restaurant would operate, and it puts the cost squarely with the business generating the grease. On paper it looks tidy - each tenant owns its own canopy, filters and the duct it can reach, and each holds its own certificate.

The trouble is that in a food court the ductwork rarely respects those neat boundaries. The moment a tenant's duct crosses into a shared riser or a communal plenum, that surface stops being anyone's obvious responsibility, and the gaps between contractors become the fire risk.

Pros

  • Cost sits with the tenant creating the grease, so heavy fryers pay for their heavier fouling rather than subsidising a flat service charge.
  • Each business keeps direct control of scheduling around its own trading hours and menu changes.
  • Clear accountability for the parts a tenant can see and touch - canopy, grease filters and the local duct drop.

Cons

  • Shared risers, communal fans and merged duct runs fall through the cracks, because no single tenant can access or owns them.
  • Frequencies drift out of step - one unit cleans quarterly, its neighbour annually, and the shared duct is only ever partly compliant.
  • A patchwork of different contractors means inconsistent TR19® Grease reporting, mismatched access panels and no single verified record for the building.
  • If a fire spreads through neglected shared ductwork, the building insurer may look at every certificate - and one weak link can put cover for the whole centre in doubt.

Option B: one centre-wide extract cleaning programme

Here the centre operator or managing agent commissions a single, coordinated programme covering every canopy, every duct run and the shared plant, with tenants brought in through their leases and the service charge. One specialist surveys the whole system, sets frequencies unit by unit, and reports against a single standard. It treats the food court as what it physically is - one connected extract network - rather than a dozen separate kitchens that happen to share a postcode.

This is the direction most large operators have moved towards, and it aligns naturally with the way fire safety in multi-occupancy food courts has to be managed as a single, building-wide strategy rather than a stack of separate ones.

Pros

  • Shared risers, plenums and communal extract fans are explicitly owned and cleaned, closing the gaps a unit-by-unit approach leaves open.
  • One survey sets risk-based frequencies across the court - quarterly for the chargrill units, annually for the sandwich bar - all documented together.
  • A single, consistent set of TR19® Grease reports, photographs and post-clean verification the fire risk assessor and insurer can actually rely on.
  • Access panels are installed to a common standard so the whole system stays inspectable, not just the easy sections.
  • Coordinated out-of-hours working means the whole court is cleaned with minimal disruption to trading.

Cons

  • Recovering cost fairly through the service charge takes careful apportionment, or light-use tenants feel they are subsidising heavy fryers.
  • It depends on lease terms and tenant cooperation - a concession can still deny access to its own canopy if the agreement is weak.
  • The operator takes on visible coordination effort and a clear duty they cannot quietly delegate away.
  • A single programme is only as good as its contractor, so specification and verification have to be tight from the outset.

Temperature and menu drive fouling as much as trading hours - high-heat frying at oil temperatures around 180°C throws far more grease into the duct than a coffee unit ever will, which is why blanket frequencies fail and unit-by-unit risk assessment wins. The goal is not a certificate for its own sake; it is keeping every internal duct surface below that grease threshold so a canopy fire cannot run the length of the building.

If your food court's shared risers and communal fans have no clear owner, a single coordinated kitchen duct cleaning programme is the cleanest way to close the gap.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Who is legally responsible for cleaning shared extract ductwork in a food court?

There is usually no single Responsible Person. Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 each catering tenant is responsible for their own canopy and local duct, while the centre operator or managing agent typically holds the shared risers, plenums and communal fans. Where duct runs merge, the law expects the various Responsible Persons to cooperate so that every internal surface is actually cleaned - which is why a coordinated programme is so effective.

How often should a food court's kitchen extract system be cleaned?

TR19 Grease bases frequency on cooking intensity rather than a single blanket rule. Heavy use such as continuous frying or chargrilling points to roughly every three months, moderate use to every six months, and light use to annually. In a food court these categories sit side by side, so the sound approach is to risk-assess each unit and clean the shared ductwork to match its most demanding contributor.

Can one tenant's poor extract cleaning affect the whole centre's insurance?

Yes. If fire spreads through neglected shared ductwork, a building insurer can examine the cleaning records for the entire system, and one missing or weak certificate can put cover in question for the whole centre. A single verified record covering every unit and the shared runs is far easier to defend than a patchwork of separate tenant certificates on different schedules.
