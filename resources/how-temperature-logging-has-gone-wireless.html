How Temperature Logging Has Gone Wireless

A single missed reading over a quiet weekend can cost you a walk-in full of stock and your due-diligence defence. Wireless temperature logging keeps the record running when nobody is there to hold the pen.

Case postmortem

The compressor gave out somewhere in the small hours of Saturday, and nobody knew a thing until the head chef opened up on Monday.

Here is a scenario we see far too often. A busy site closes on Friday night. The walk-in is packed for the weekend - trays of prepped proteins, dairy, a full section of ready-to-eat items. The last person out does the usual sweep, ticks the fridge log on the clipboard by the door, and locks up. At some point over the weekend the unit trips. The air inside climbs slowly, then steadily, and by the time anyone returns the walk-in is sitting well above the legal limit. The paper log tells you nothing, because the paper log only knows what someone wrote on it, and no one was there to write.

The chef has to make a call with no information. How long has it been warm? Did it hit temperature at midnight or at ten this morning? Was anything above 8°C for two hours or for thirty? With no answers, the only defensible decision is to bin the lot. Thousands of pounds of stock into the waste, a scramble to re-order, and a very uncomfortable conversation when the environmental health officer next asks to see the records. That single gap in the log is the whole story, and it is exactly the gap that wireless temperature logging closes.

For decades, temperature control in a commercial kitchen meant a clipboard and a pen. Someone reads the dial or the probe, writes a number in a box twice a day, and moves on. It works right up until the moment it matters most - overnight, at the weekend, on the one shift where the reading got skipped because service was slammed. The law does not care that you were busy. Cold food that needs chilling must be held at or below 8°C under the Food Safety (Temperature Control) Regulations 1995, and the Food Standards Agency recommends you run fridges at 5°C or lower to leave headroom for the door opening and the odd defrost cycle. Hot holding sits at 63°C and above. The danger zone in between is where pathogens multiply, and in a warm kitchen "too long" can be as little as two hours.

Wireless logging changes the question from "did someone remember to check" to "what actually happened, minute by minute". A small sensor sits inside each fridge, freezer or hot-holding unit and records the air temperature at set intervals around the clock. The readings travel over the site network to a dashboard, and if any unit drifts past its threshold the system pushes an alert to a phone - at 2am on a Sunday if that is when the compressor dies. Instead of discovering a failure two days late, you get a text while there is still time to move stock, prop a door, or call the engineer out. Modern units running carbon dioxide (CO₂) refrigerant are efficient but still fail like anything else, and the value of the system is that it never sleeps and never gets distracted by a rush.

What went wrong

Pick the weekend failure apart and the paper system fails at three separate points, none of which are the fault of the staff.

  • No cover out of hours. A manual log can only record when a human is present. Between the Friday close and the Monday open there was nobody to take a reading, so the single most important stretch of time - the unattended weekend - is a blank. The failure happened precisely in the window the system could not see.
  • No timeline to lean on. Even once the warm walk-in was discovered, there was no way to know when it went warm. Due diligence under the Food Safety Act 1990 rests on being able to show what you knew and when you acted. Without a timestamped record, the business cannot prove the food was safe, so every item has to be treated as unsafe. That is a defensible decision, but an expensive one.
  • No early warning. A compressor rarely dies without a hint. A unit that creeps up a degree over three days is often a bearing or a seal on the way out. On paper, two readings a day round to the nearest whole number and that drift hides in plain sight. The trend that would have flagged the fault a week early was never visible, because the data was too coarse and too sparse to show it.

The honest summary is that the clipboard did its job perfectly and still let the kitchen down, because its job was only ever to record what a person saw. The moment the risk moved outside human hours, the record went dark - and a dark record is indistinguishable, to an inspector, from no record at all.

The fix

Going wireless is not about buying a gadget and hoping. Done properly it is a small project, and the order of the steps matters. Here is how we would approach it.

  1. Map every controlled point first. Walk the site and list each fridge, walk-in, freezer, blast chiller and hot-holding unit. Note which hold ready-to-eat food and which are the highest risk. This tells you how many sensors you need and where the alerts must be loudest.
  2. Choose recorders to a known standard. Look for loggers built to BS EN 12830:2018, the European standard for temperature recorders in cold-chain storage. The 2018 revision added a higher accuracy class of ±0.2°C across the −25°C to +7°C range, tighter than the ±0.5°C of the old version. That accuracy is what makes the record stand up.
  3. Set thresholds with headroom. Do not alarm at the 8°C legal line. Set a warning band a degree or two below so you get told while there is still time to act, not once you are already in breach. Freezers alarm below −18°C; hot holding below 63°C.
  4. Decide who gets the alert, and when. An alarm nobody receives is worthless. Route out-of-hours alerts to the duty manager and a named deputy, and agree the corrective steps in advance - log it, move stock, call the engineer, record the outcome.
  5. Keep the records tidy and exportable. The system should generate its own reports so a HACCP audit or an EHO visit is a two-minute export, not an afternoon of hunting for a clipboard. Businesses moving off paper typically cut compliance admin by 40 to 60 per cent.
  6. Calibrate and verify on a schedule. A sensor that has quietly drifted is worse than no sensor, because you trust it. Build in periodic calibration checks against a reference probe and keep the certificates.

Where cleaning and monitoring meet

A sensor reads the air, but the equipment around it has to earn that reading. Grease-laden coils and blocked condensers make a fridge work harder and run warmer, and a filthy canopy pushes heat back into the space your chillers are fighting. Wireless data is at its most useful when the kit is genuinely clean, because then a rising trend means a real fault rather than a maintenance backlog. The same logic applies to your extraction system - a clean, well-serviced kitchen is one where the numbers can be trusted. It is also worth learning how to audit the work a contractor has done so that calibration certificates and service reports are checked, not just filed.

The same discipline that protects a walk-in also protects a long counter - see our guide on how to keep food at temperature across a long service counter.

None of this replaces good habits. Staff still need to probe cooked and reheated food, still need to know the danger zone, and still need to act when an alarm sounds. What wireless logging removes is the single point of failure that sank the weekend walk-in - the reliance on a person being present at the exact moment something goes wrong. The record now runs whether the kitchen is full or empty, lit or dark. When the inspector asks what happened at 3am on a Sunday, you have an answer, and it is on a screen rather than a shrug.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Is wireless temperature monitoring a legal requirement in a UK kitchen?

No, the law does not specify wireless systems. The Food Safety (Temperature Control) Regulations 1995 require you to hold chilled food at or below 8 degrees C, and you must be able to evidence that you did. Wireless logging is simply the most reliable way to produce that evidence around the clock, which is why so many businesses now use it to underpin their due-diligence defence.

What accuracy should I look for in a temperature logger?

Look for recorders built to BS EN 12830:2018, the European standard for cold-chain temperature recorders. The 2018 revision introduced a higher accuracy class of plus or minus 0.2 degrees C across the minus 25 to plus 7 degrees C range, tighter than the plus or minus 0.5 degrees C of the older standard. Whatever you choose, calibrate it periodically against a reference probe and keep the certificates.

Does wireless monitoring mean my staff can stop checking food temperatures?

No. Sensors read the air inside a unit, not the core of a cooked or reheated dish. Your team still needs to probe food, understand the 8 to 63 degrees C danger zone, and act on alerts. Wireless logging removes the reliance on someone being present overnight; it does not remove the need for trained staff during service.
