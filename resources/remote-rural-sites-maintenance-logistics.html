Remote and Rural Sites: Maintenance Logistics

Distance does not change what the standards ask of you - it changes how you get the work done, evidenced and signed off. Here is how to keep a remote or rural kitchen extraction system compliant without the cost and downtime spiralling.

Remote & rural sites

The standards do not move for you

A commercial kitchen an hour from the nearest depot is held to exactly the same rules as one in the middle of a city - the difference is entirely in the logistics of getting the work done, verified and recorded.

If you run catering on a rural estate, a country hotel, a holiday park, a distribution hub or an off-grid site, you already know the tax that distance puts on everything. A single missed part means another day lost. A contractor who quotes cheerfully by phone goes quiet when they see the postcode. And the temptation, when a visit is hard to arrange, is to stretch the interval and hope nobody asks.

The problem is that grease does not care how far you are from anyone. It builds at the same rate over your cookline whether the flue vents onto a busy street or open fields, and a ductwork fire spreads just as fast. So the honest goal for a remote site is not to do less - it is to plan tighter, consolidate visits, and make sure every attendance leaves you with the evidence you would need in front of an insurer or a fire officer.

Why distance changes the maintenance equation

On a city site, a contractor can pop back. A forgotten access panel, a filter left off, a re-test after a repair - none of it is a crisis, because they are twenty minutes away tomorrow. On a remote site every one of those small omissions becomes an expensive return journey, or worse, a compliance gap that sits open until the next scheduled visit months later.

That shifts where the effort has to go. Instead of managing the clean, you are managing the trip. Three things tend to decide whether a remote visit succeeds or fails.

Get everything done in one attendance

The single biggest saving on a distant site is refusing to split work across visits that did not need splitting. If a team is already travelling to you, mobilising vans, ladders, access equipment and pressure systems, then the kitchen extract clean, the filter changes, the fan servicing and - where it applies - the LEV thorough examination should be scoped to happen together, or in a tight sequence, rather than as separate call-outs weeks apart. Every extra mobilisation is travel time and cost you pay for whether the work takes an hour or a day.

Confirm site conditions before anyone sets off

Nothing wastes a long journey like arriving to find you cannot start. For extraction cleaning that usually means water and power for the wash-down and vacuum plant, safe roof or height access to the fan and cowl, parking close enough to run hoses and cables, and a kitchen that is cold and out of service for the window booked. On sites with their own power or water constraints, or shared access tracks, this needs confirming in writing beforehand - not discovered at 7am. A pre-visit checklist shared with your maintenance supplier list removes most of the surprises that turn a one-day job into two.

Book the right people, not just anyone nearby

Rural coverage is thin, and it is tempting to take whoever will travel. But kitchen extract work carries real competence requirements. Since the TR19 Grease specification was published by BESA in 2019, the technician carrying out and signing off the clean should hold the Grease Hygiene Technician (GHT) qualification, and any LEV thorough examination must be done by someone meeting the HSG258 competence criteria, typically holding BOHS-recognised training such as P601. Distance is not a reason to drop those standards - it is a reason to plan around fewer, better-qualified visits.

The numbers that drive the schedule

What compliance actually requires

Before you can plan the logistics, you need to know how often the standards expect you on site and what each visit has to prove. These are the figures that set your rhythm - and none of them relax because you are hard to reach.

3 · 6 · 12
TR19 Grease cleaning bands in months - heavy use over 12 hours a day, moderate use, and light use
200 µm

14 months
Legal maximum interval for an LEV thorough examination and test under COSHH, with records kept at least five years

Those cleaning bands come from how hard the kitchen works, not where it sits. A busy site running the cookline more than twelve hours a day falls into the roughly quarterly band; a moderate-use kitchen sits around six-monthly; light use can be annual. The interval is set by measured grease loading, using a Deposit Thickness Test or Wet Film Thickness Test at points along the ductwork, so the honest way to justify a longer interval on a remote site is evidence that the grease is genuinely building slowly - not the fact that you would rather not schedule the visit.

The LEV figure is a hard legal ceiling, not a target. If your site also has a separate local exhaust ventilation system - a spray booth, a fume arm, a dust extraction rig alongside the kitchen - that fourteen-month examination is a statutory maximum under COSHH, and higher-risk processes may need it sooner. Missing it because the engineer was hard to get to is still a breach.

Planning the visit so it only happens once

Once you know your intervals, remote-site maintenance becomes a scheduling exercise. The aim is a predictable calendar of consolidated visits, each one fully evidenced, so nothing forces an unplanned return.

Build the year backwards from your cleaning band. If you are on a roughly quarterly clean, fix those dates well ahead, then hang everything else that needs a visit - fan servicing, filter stock, any LEV examination, minor repairs flagged last time - onto the same attendances wherever the timing allows. Order consumables and any likely spares to arrive with the team, so a worn belt or a damaged access panel gasket does not trigger a second trip. Treat access equipment the same way: if reaching the fan needs a tower or a roof harness point, that gets arranged in the booking, not improvised on arrival.

Then insist on the evidence pack, because on a remote site the report is what protects you between visits. A proper TR19 Grease post-clean record should give you before-and-after photographs from matching positions, deposit measurements confirming grease has been brought down below the verified threshold, a written summary of the works, notes on any areas the team could not reach, system drawings marking what was cleaned, and a dated hygiene certificate naming the qualified technician. That pack is what an insurer or fire officer will ask to see, and it is what tells you honestly whether your interval is right or whether the grease is building faster than the schedule assumes. Access limitations noted in one report become the first fix scoped into the next visit - which is exactly how you stop small gaps compounding on a site you cannot easily revisit.

Finally, price it properly. Remote work carries real travel, mobilisation and sometimes overnight cost, and pretending otherwise just produces quotes that collapse or corners that get cut. Building those realities into a planned figure, rather than absorbing them as emergency call-outs, is far cheaper over a year.

If distant sites keep throwing up costs you did not see coming, it is worth learning how to build a maintenance budget that prevents surprises.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Can we clean a remote kitchen extract system less often to save on travel?

No - the cleaning interval is set by how much grease your system actually builds, not by how far away the site is. TR19 Grease expects the mean deposit to stay below 200 microns between cleans, measured by a Deposit Thickness or Wet Film Thickness Test. If the measurements show grease building slowly you may genuinely justify a longer interval, but distance alone is never the reason.

How do we get everything done in a single visit to avoid repeat journeys?

Scope all the work that needs a site attendance into one booking - the extract clean, filter changes, fan servicing and any LEV examination - and confirm site conditions in writing first. Make sure water, power, height access and parking are all available, and have consumables and likely spares delivered to arrive with the team. This stops a small missing part turning into a second long trip.

Does a rural site still need a qualified TR19 technician?

Yes. The person carrying out and signing off the clean should hold the BESA Grease Hygiene Technician (GHT) qualification, and any LEV thorough examination must be done by someone meeting the HSG258 competence criteria, usually with BOHS-recognised P601 training. Thin local coverage is a reason to plan fewer, better visits - not to lower the standard.

What paperwork should we insist on after each visit?

A full TR19 Grease post-clean pack: before-and-after photographs from matching positions, deposit measurements confirming the grease is below the verified threshold, a written summary of works, notes on any areas that could not be reached, system drawings showing what was cleaned, and a dated hygiene certificate naming the technician. For LEV, keep the thorough examination records for at least five years. On a remote site this pack is your evidence between visits.
