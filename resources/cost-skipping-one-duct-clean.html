The Cost of Skipping One Duct Clean

Putting off one kitchen duct clean looks like an easy saving on a tight month. Here is what that decision can actually cost you when grease keeps building behind the panels.

The false economy

A duct clean is one of those jobs that never feels urgent - right up until the moment it does.

When the diary is full and the margins are thin, deferring one scheduled clean feels like a sensible bit of housekeeping. The kitchen still fires up, the extract fan still pulls, and nobody standing at the pass can see the layer of grease thickening inside the ductwork above the canopy. That is exactly the problem. The risk you are carrying is invisible until it is a fire, a failed inspection, or a rejected insurance claim.

This page weighs the two choices honestly - skipping the clean against staying on schedule - so you can see what each one really costs. Grease-laden vapour rises off every range and fryer, condenses on the cool metal of the duct run, and sets into a hard, flammable coating. The industry standard that governs all of this in the UK is TR19® Grease, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA), and it is unusually specific about how much grease is too much. Understanding it is the difference between a managed cost and a gamble.

Option A: Skipping the clean

Deferring a clean is not irrational. It saves a real, immediate outlay and it frees the kitchen from a half-day of disruption. The trouble is that every benefit sits in the present and every cost sits in the future, where it grows.

What you gain

  • An immediate saving on this quarter's cleaning invoice, which helps in a lean trading month.
  • No shutdown of the extract system and no half-day working around a crew above the canopy.
  • The kitchen carries on exactly as before, so nothing appears to break.

What it costs you

  • Grease keeps loading. TR19® Grease sets a mean deposit ceiling of 200 microns - roughly half the thickness of a business card - and once you pass it the run is legally overdue and materially more dangerous.
  • Fire risk climbs sharply. Around 70% of commercial kitchen fires start in the extract system because of built-up fat and grease, and the London Fire Brigade records a kitchen extract duct fire in the capital roughly every nine days.
  • Your insurance quietly weakens. The Association of British Insurers puts payouts on fires caused by poorly maintained extract ducts at around £65 million a year, and without a valid post-clean certificate an insurer can refuse a claim outright, leaving you to fund the rebuild and lost trade yourself.
  • You risk falling foul of the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, under which the duty to control this hazard sits squarely with you as the responsible person.
  • The next clean gets harder. Hardened, carbonised grease takes longer to shift and can need a deposit thickness test rather than a simple wet film check, so the bill you deferred comes back larger.
  • The saving is a loan against yourself, and the interest is a fire you cannot see coming.

Option B: Staying on the schedule

Keeping to a planned cleaning frequency turns an unpredictable hazard into a fixed, budgeted line. It costs more across the year than skipping - but it is a known cost, and it is the only version of this decision that leaves you covered when something goes wrong.

What you gain

  • Documented compliance. Each visit produces a post-clean verification report - before-and-after photographs from matching positions, wet film thickness readings, and a certificate naming the qualified technician and the date.
  • A valid insurance position. That paperwork is exactly what an insurer asks to see after a fire, so your cover stays intact rather than becoming a dispute.
  • Grease held well below the threshold. A proper clean takes the average deposit back under 50 microns across the system, giving you real headroom before the 200 micron ceiling returns.
  • A frequency matched to your usage, not guesswork. TR19® Grease sets heavy-use kitchens (12 to 16 hours a day) at quarterly, moderate use (6 to 12 hours) at half-yearly, and light use (2 to 6 hours) at least annually.
  • A cleaner, better-drawing extract system, which helps the whole kitchen run cooler and more efficiently.

What it costs you

  • A recurring, planned outlay across the year rather than a one-off you can dodge.
  • Scheduled downtime for each visit, which needs a slot in the rota - usually a quiet morning or a closed day.
  • Access work if the ductwork is short on inspection hatches, since TR19® Grease expects the whole run to be reachable and verifiable, not just the easy stretches.
A hard-working fryer line is one of the biggest grease sources feeding your ducts, which is also why fryers cost more to run than their menu price suggests.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Will my insurer really refuse a claim if I miss one duct clean?

They can, and it happens. Most commercial policies require you to maintain the extract system to a recognised standard such as TR19 Grease, and after a fire an insurer will ask for your post-clean verification certificates. If the run was overdue and you cannot produce a valid, dated report from a qualified technician, the insurer is within its rights to reduce or reject the claim, which can leave you funding the repairs and lost trade yourself.

How often does my kitchen extract system actually need cleaning?

TR19 Grease ties frequency to how hard the kitchen works. Heavy use of 12 to 16 hours a day calls for quarterly cleaning, moderate use of 6 to 12 hours for half-yearly, and light use of 2 to 6 hours for at least annual cleaning. The underlying rule is that the mean grease deposit must never exceed 200 microns between cleans, so a busy site with a lot of frying may need attention sooner than the headline band suggests.

What proof do I get that the clean was done properly?

A compliant clean produces a post-clean verification report, not just an invoice. It should include before-and-after photographs taken from matching positions, wet film or deposit thickness readings confirming grease is back below the threshold, a summary of the work and any access limitations, and a hygiene certificate stating the date, the location and the name of the technician. That document is what protects your insurance position and satisfies a fire risk assessment.
