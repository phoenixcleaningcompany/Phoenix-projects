Why Do My Kitchen Drains Smell?

A drain smell that lingers after a full clean is almost never about how hard you scrubbed - it is about what is happening below the floor. Here is how commercial kitchen drains turn sour, and the order you should tackle it in.

Case postmortem

The kitchen was spotless. Every surface had been wiped, the floor mopped and squeegeed, the bins emptied - and yet by the middle of Friday service a sour, eggy smell was drifting up from the floor gully near the dishwash, strong enough that the closest table sent a plate back and asked to move.

The head chef did what most people do first. He poured a kettle of boiling water and a slug of bleach down the gully, and for about twenty minutes it worked. Then the smell came back, worse, because all he had really done was strip out what little water was sitting in the trap and pour a fresh problem on top of an old one. By the time we were called in, the team had convinced themselves the drains needed replacing. They did not. Nothing was broken - the system was simply doing exactly what a neglected drain does, and every quick fix had made it a little worse.

This is one of the most common calls we take, and the pattern almost never varies. A clean kitchen still smells of drains because the smell was never coming from the parts you can see and scrub. It is coming from the trap, the gully and the grease sitting in the pipe run - and until you understand what went wrong down there, you will keep chasing it with bleach and losing.

What went wrong

Every drain in your kitchen relies on one small thing to keep sewer air out of the room - a water seal. Where the pipe leaves a sink, a gully or a floor drain, it dips down and back up in a U-bend, and the pool of water trapped in that bend physically blocks the gases coming up from the drainage system below. Under the Building Regulations, Approved Document H1 (foul water drainage), that seal is meant to be 50mm deep for a sink and 75mm for a WC, and it must never fall below 25mm of retained water in normal use. When the seal is there, the room stays fresh. When it drops, the drain breathes straight into your kitchen.

In this case three things had gone wrong at once, which is typical.

The trap had run dry. The floor gully by the dishwash only saw water when someone happened to tip a mop bucket near it. On a quiet week the seal evaporated, and once the water was gone there was nothing between the room and the sewer. That is the eggy note people describe - hydrogen sulphide, H₂S, produced as waste breaks down without oxygen further down the line.

The pipe run was lined with grease. Fat, oil and grease - FOG - leaves the kitchen as warm liquid and travels a metre or two before it cools, hardens and clings to the pipe wall. Over months it narrows the bore, traps food solids and rots in place. This is not just a smell problem. The Water Industry Act 1991 makes it an offence to discharge anything into a public sewer that blocks the free flow of its contents, and FOG is the single biggest cause of that. Businesses found to be feeding blockages can face fines running into tens of thousands of pounds.

The bleach made it worse. Chlorine bleach does not clear a grease-lined pipe - the fat is not water-soluble, so it slides straight past. What the bleach did do was react with the organic matter already sitting in the gully and add its own harsh top note, while the boiling water melted a little grease further along only for it to set again in a colder section of pipe. Every quick fix had moved the problem, not removed it.

The short version

If you strip everything else away, a smelly kitchen drain almost always comes down to three numbers worth remembering.

50mm
Minimum water seal depth for a sink trap under Approved Document H1 - lose it and sewer gas enters the room.
25mm
The least water a trap may retain in normal use before the seal is judged to have failed.
1–2 months
How often a busy kitchen's grease separator typically needs emptying to stop FOG rotting in the line.

None of those numbers are about cleaning harder. They are about the drainage system being intact and maintained, which is a different job from your daily close-down - and the reason a faultlessly cleaned kitchen can still smell. If your team scrubs everything and the odour returns within the hour, the fault is below the floor, not on it. We cover that specific frustration in more detail in why a kitchen still smells after a full daily clean.

The fix

The order matters here. Restoring the seals first tells you how much of the smell was simply dry traps, and stops you spending money on a deep clean the drain did not need. Work through it like this.

  1. Recharge every trap and gully. Pour a jug of clean water slowly into each floor gully, dry-looking drain and rarely-used sink until the U-bend is full again. In many cases the smell drops within minutes, which tells you evaporation was the main culprit and gives you a clean baseline to judge the rest.
  2. Find the traps that keep drying out. Any gully that empties itself between services needs a reason to stay wet - either it is plumbed to catch waste water it currently misses, or it needs a trap gully guard or a self-sealing waste valve fitted so the seal holds when the water is not flowing. A drainage engineer can advise which suits each point.
  3. Clear the grease from the pipe run, not just the surface. Bleach and boiling water will not touch a hardened FOG lining. The line needs mechanical cleaning and hot flushing back to a clean bore, so waste flows freely and stops rotting in place. This is skilled work and worth doing properly once rather than badly every fortnight.
  4. Service or install grease separation. Drainage serving a commercial hot-food kitchen should pass through grease removal complying with BS EN 1825 and PDI G101 before it reaches the sewer. If you have a separator, get it emptied and maintained on a schedule - roughly every one to two months for a busy site. If you do not, this is the point to fit one. Our guide to grease trap management walks through sizing, emptying intervals and the records you should keep.
  5. Put maintenance on a calendar. Once the system is clean and sealed, it stays that way with a light routine - flushing quiet gullies weekly, logging separator empties, and having the pipe run cleaned periodically before FOG can rebuild. Reactive bleach-and-hope is what got the drain into trouble in the first place.
If the smell keeps coming back after you have recharged the traps, book a drainage and deep clean assessment so the grease line and separator get dealt with properly - talk to us about a commercial kitchen deep clean.

What to stop doing today

Two habits keep this problem alive. The first is pouring bleach down a smelly drain - it masks nothing for long and adds its own odour. The second is scraping pans and tipping fat-heavy waste water straight into the sink or gully. Fat belongs in a sealed container in the bin or in your used-oil collection, never down the drain, because every litre that goes down is a litre lining your pipes. Fix those two things and most kitchens never smell again once the system itself has been cleaned.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Why does my kitchen drain smell even after a thorough clean?

Because the smell is coming from below the floor, not the surfaces you can scrub. The usual causes are a dried-out trap letting sewer gas into the room, or a grease-lined pipe run rotting in place. Neither is affected by mopping and wiping, which is why a spotless kitchen can still smell within the hour. Recharge every gully with water first, and if the odour returns the pipe run needs cleaning.

Will pouring bleach down the drain get rid of the smell?

Not for long, and it often makes things worse. Fat, oil and grease are not water-soluble, so bleach slides straight past the hardened lining that actually causes the smell. It also strips the water seal from the trap and adds its own harsh odour when it reacts with waste in the gully. The lasting fix is to restore the trap seals and mechanically clean the grease from the pipe.

Do I legally need a grease trap in my commercial kitchen?

There is no single law that names grease traps as compulsory, but the Water Industry Act 1991 makes it an offence to discharge fat, oil and grease that blocks a public sewer, and guidance points to grease separation meeting BS EN 1825 and PDI G101. In practice that means effective grease removal is expected, and businesses feeding blockages can face substantial fines. A properly sized and regularly emptied separator is the straightforward way to stay compliant.
