Heritage Pubs: Ventilation Without Wrecking the Character

A heritage pub can carry a hard-working kitchen and keep every ounce of its character - if the extraction is planned around the fabric rather than bolted onto it.

The fabric comes first, the ductwork second

An old pub earns its living from atmosphere, and the quickest way to spoil that is a clumsy stainless flue nailed across a historic elevation.

The good news is that a busy grill and a protected building are not really in conflict. Most of the friction comes from treating ventilation as an afterthought - a fan and a duct chosen on the back of a fit-out, then squeezed through whatever wall is nearest the kitchen. Plan it the other way round, starting from the structure and the neighbours, and you can hit every safety and hygiene standard while leaving the character untouched. What follows is the order of work we use on listed and historically sensitive sites, so nothing irreversible happens before the right people have looked at it.

A sequence that protects both the building and the kitchen

  1. Survey the historic fabric before you draw a single duct run. Get a measured look at wall thicknesses, timber frames, chimney breasts and any concealed decorative plaster, because these decide where a duct can honestly go and where it cannot. On a listed building the aim is to route services through voids, redundant flues and existing openings so that nothing original is cut, drilled or lost. This early survey is also your evidence base if consent is later queried.
  2. Bring the conservation officer in early, not at the objection stage. New external flues, extract terminals and plant almost always need listed building consent, and street-facing elevations are the hardest to win. A pre-application conversation lets you test discreet options - a rear termination, a reused chimney, a colour-matched cowl - before money is committed. Our wider notes on managing ventilation in listed and heritage buildings go through what officers typically ask for.
  3. Route the extract to respect the elevation. Wherever possible, take the duct up through an existing chimney stack or an internal void and terminate high and out of sight, rather than penetrating a visible wall. Reusing a redundant flue is often the single move that turns a refusal into an approval, because it adds nothing new to the skyline. Where a new penetration is unavoidable, keep it small, keep it to a secondary elevation, and match the finish to the surrounding stone or render.
  4. Size and specify for the cooking, not the fashion. Extract rates should follow the canopy and the appliance line under EN 16282 and DW/172, so the system actually captures the grease-laden air a heavy pub grill produces. Undersizing leaves smoke and heat in the room; oversizing wastes energy and pulls warmth straight out of the bar. Get the volume right first and the ducting can then be as compact and well-hidden as the fabric demands.
  5. Design out the noise and the odour at the same time. Old pubs sit cheek by jowl with homes, so a planning odour and noise assessment - often measured against BS 4142 - is frequently the deciding factor. Larger fans running slowly, in-duct silencers, acoustic enclosures and a well-placed cowl all cut the nuisance without a bigger visual footprint. Our guidance on acoustic design for kitchen ventilation covers how to keep a late-night kitchen quiet for the flat upstairs.
  6. Build in proper access for cleaning from day one. TR19 Grease requires access panels of sufficient number, size and quality that the whole run can be inspected and cleaned, and retrofitting them into a listed wall later is painful and destructive. Position removable panels at every bend and at sensible intervals along straight runs while the ceilings are open. This one decision decides whether the system can ever be kept compliant.
  7. Set a realistic cleaning frequency and record it. A hard-working pub kitchen running twelve to sixteen hours is usually a three-monthly clean under TR19 Grease, with lighter operations less often. Agree the interval against the measured grease load rather than guesswork, and diarise it so it never slips. The cheapest way to protect an irreplaceable building from a grease fire is simply to clean the duct before the deposit builds.
  8. Commission, document and hand over a compliance file. Balance the system, verify the airflow, and issue a post-clean report with before-and-after deposit readings and photographs. Keep the listed building consent, the drawings, the access-panel schedule and every cleaning certificate together in one place. That file is what satisfies your insurer, your fire risk assessor and the next licensee alike.

Why the standards and the heritage pull in the same direction

It is tempting to see conservation rules and fire-safety rules as opposing forces, one wanting the system invisible and the other wanting it accessible. In practice they reward the same discipline. TR19 Grease, the BESA specification issued in 2019 for managing fire risk from grease accumulation, expects the mean deposit across the system to stay below 200 microns, treats any single reading over 500 microns as needing immediate localised cleaning, and looks for post-clean verification down below the standard's clean threshold using a deposit thickness test. You can only meet that if the duct was designed to be reached - and a duct designed to be reached is also a duct that was routed thoughtfully through the fabric rather than smashed through it.

The legal weight sits behind this too. Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 the responsible person must manage the fire risk that grease in the extract represents, and insurers increasingly ask for evidence of compliant cleaning before they will pay out on a fire claim. For a heritage pub the stakes are higher than a modern unit, because what burns cannot be rebuilt. A grease fire that starts in a neglected duct does not respect a Grade listing, so the documentation you build up is as much a conservation measure as a compliance one.

There is a craft point in all of this. The best heritage installations are the ones you never notice - the flue you assume was always the chimney, the plant you cannot hear from the beer garden, the canopy that clears the grill without a whisper reaching the snug. That standard of invisibility is not achieved by cutting corners on the extract; it is achieved by getting the engineering exactly right so that it can afford to disappear. Character and capability are not a trade-off. They are the same job done properly.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I need listed building consent to install a kitchen extract flue in a heritage pub?

Almost always, yes. New external flues, terminals and plant affect a listed building's appearance, so they need consent, and street-facing elevations are the hardest to get approved. Speak to the conservation officer before the design is fixed - reusing a redundant chimney or terminating on a secondary elevation is often what turns a likely refusal into an approval.

How often does a busy pub kitchen extract system need cleaning?

A pub grill running twelve to sixteen hours a day is typically a three-monthly clean under TR19 Grease, while lighter kitchens can go longer. The honest answer comes from measuring the grease load rather than guessing. The specification looks for the mean deposit to stay below 200 microns, so the interval should be set to keep you comfortably under that between visits.

Can extraction be fitted in a historic building without damaging original fabric?

Yes, if the fabric is surveyed first and the duct is routed through existing voids, redundant flues and current openings rather than new penetrations. The aim is that nothing original is cut or lost, and that access panels for future cleaning go in while ceilings are open. Done this way the system meets every hygiene and fire standard while staying practically invisible.
