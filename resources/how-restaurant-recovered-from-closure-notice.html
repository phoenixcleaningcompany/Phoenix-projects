How a Restaurant Recovered From a Closure Notice

A closure notice is a hard public stop, but it is recoverable. Here is how one commercial kitchen reopened by fixing what the officer found, proving it with evidence, and never letting it build back up.

Case study

A closure notice is not the end of a kitchen - but it is a hard, public stop, and how an operator responds in the first few days usually decides whether they reopen inside a week or lose a fortnight of trade.

The kitchen in this account was a busy independent doing long service most days of the week. One routine visit from the local authority turned into a served notice, a locked door and a sign in the window. What follows is how the site got back open - not through arguing, but by fixing the thing the officer had actually found, proving it with evidence, and putting a schedule in place so it could never quietly build back up. Names and locations are left out on purpose; the mechanics are what matter, and they are the same for almost any commercial kitchen in the country.

What was actually on the table

Before any cleaning could be planned, it helped to be clear about what the notice was, what it demanded, and where the underlying risk had been hiding. Three short lists set the scene.

What a closure notice really is

  • A Hygiene Emergency Prohibition Notice is served under Section 12 of the Food Safety Act 1990 when an environmental health officer judges there is an imminent risk of injury to health.
  • It takes effect immediately - the premises must stop trading and the notice is fixed conspicuously where the public can see it.
  • In England, Wales and Northern Ireland the authority must apply to a magistrates court within three days to confirm the notice as a Hygiene Emergency Prohibition Order.
  • Trading on in breach of the notice is a criminal offence, carrying an unlimited fine and up to two years imprisonment - so reopening early was never an option.
  • The prohibition is only lifted once the officer has re-inspected and is satisfied the health risk is gone, then issues a certificate to that effect.

Where the risk was hiding

  • The visible kitchen was not the whole story - the officer's concern reached into the extract canopy, the filters and the ductwork carrying grease-laden air up to the fan and roof.
  • Around 70% of fires in commercial kitchens start in the extraction system, where fat and grease bake onto duct walls out of sight.
  • Under the BESA TR19® Grease specification, grease should never exceed a mean of 200 microns across the system between cleans, and any single point above 500 microns needs urgent attention.
  • Left long enough, that build-up is both a fire hazard and a hygiene one - a warm, greasy surface feeding straight back over the cooking line.
  • A canopy that looks wiped and tidy can still hide a duct run that has not been opened or measured in years.

What reopening was going to require

  • A full deep clean of the cooking suite, surfaces, floors and drainage, plus the extract canopy, filters, plenum and accessible ductwork back to the fan.
  • Post-clean verification to TR19® Grease - deposit thickness or wet film testing to prove grease was brought back below 50 microns as an average across the system.
  • A written verification report with before-and-after photographs from matching positions, a hygiene certificate, and the name and qualification of the technician who did the work.
  • Work carried out or led by a technician holding the BESA Grease Hygiene Technician qualification, so the evidence would stand up if it was ever questioned.
  • A clear account for the officer of what had changed, backed by receipts, photographs and the certificate, ahead of the re-inspection.

How the recovery actually went

The first phone call, understandably, was panicked. The useful thing to say early on is that a closure is recoverable and that the clock is shorter than it feels - the barrier to reopening is not paperwork or persuasion, it is physically removing the hazard the officer found and being able to show, with numbers, that it is gone.

The site was attended the same day for a survey. That meant getting inside the canopy and opening the duct at access points rather than judging the system from the floor. The cooking line itself was tired but not the real issue; the ductwork was. Grease had built into a firm, dark layer well past the point where a wipe-down does anything, and in one vertical run the deposit was heavy enough to be an obvious fire risk on its own. That survey mattered for a second reason - it produced the baseline photographs and measurements that would later prove how far the clean had moved things.

The deep clean ran over a small number of days rather than a single overnight shift, because doing it properly meant stripping and degreasing the canopy and filters, cleaning the plenum, and working the full accessible length of the duct to the fan - not just the sections you can reach from a step. Behind the cooking line, equipment was pulled out so the walls, floor and gullies underneath could be degreased and sanitised, since those hidden faces are exactly where an officer looks on a return visit. The fabric of the kitchen - extract, surfaces, drainage, storage - was brought back to a standard the operator could stand next to while the officer inspected.

Verification was the part that turned a good clean into a reopening. Once the system was clean, deposit thickness testing was carried out at points along the ductwork and the readings recorded, confirming grease was back below the TR19® Grease post-clean threshold. Those readings went into a verification report alongside the matched before-and-after photographs, a plan of what had been cleaned, notes on any panel that could not be reached, and a dated hygiene certificate naming the technician. That report did two jobs at once - it gave the environmental health officer concrete evidence for the re-inspection, and it gave the operator's insurer the compliant, documented clean that many policies now quietly require. Where a claim after a grease fire can be challenged on the strength of cleaning records, a proper report is the difference between being covered and being exposed.

The re-inspection was straightforward precisely because nothing was being hidden. The officer could see the work, read the numbers and cross-check the photographs, and the certificate confirming the risk was removed followed. The doors reopened. The quieter cost was the human one - a closure rattles a team, and good chefs and front-of-house staff do not always wait around through an uncertain shutdown. The knock-on expense of replacing them can dwarf the cost of the clean itself, which is worth weighing when you read about the true cost of staff turnover in a restaurant; keeping the site compliant and open is also how you keep the people who make it work.

The last piece was making sure this never happened twice. TR19® Grease ties cleaning frequency to how hard the kitchen runs - roughly every three months for heavy use of twelve to sixteen hours a day, every six months for moderate use, and at least annually for lighter cooking. The site went onto a scheduled programme matched to its hours, with verification and certification each visit, so grease is measured and cleared long before it approaches the 200 micron limit. That turns compliance from a crisis into a diary entry - and means the evidence is always current if an officer, an insurer or a landlord ever asks to see it.

Questions

Frequently asked questions

What is a Hygiene Emergency Prohibition Notice and how fast does it take effect?

It is a notice served under Section 12 of the Food Safety Act 1990 when an environmental health officer judges there is an imminent risk of injury to health. It takes effect immediately, so the premises must stop trading at once and display the notice. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland the authority must then apply to a magistrates court within three days to confirm it as an order.

How quickly can a kitchen reopen after a closure notice?

There is no fixed period - the premises stays closed until an officer re-inspects and is satisfied the health risk has been removed, then issues a certificate. In practice that turns on how fast the actual hazard is fixed and evidenced. A site that deep cleans thoroughly, verifies the work and presents clear records can often be re-inspected and reopened within days rather than weeks.

Why did the extraction system matter so much to reopening?

Grease-laden extract ductwork is both a serious fire risk and a hygiene one, and around 70 percent of commercial kitchen fires start there. Under the BESA TR19 Grease specification, grease should not exceed a mean of 200 microns across the system between cleans. A canopy can look clean from the floor while the hidden duct run behind it is well over that limit, which is exactly what an officer is trained to check.

What evidence proves a kitchen extract clean was done properly?

A compliant TR19 Grease post-clean verification report. It should include deposit thickness or wet film measurements confirming grease is back below 50 microns on average, before-and-after photographs from matching positions, a summary of the work and any areas that could not be accessed, and a dated hygiene certificate naming the qualified technician. That report satisfies the environmental health officer and supports any insurance claim after a fire.
