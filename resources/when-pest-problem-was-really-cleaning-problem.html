When a Pest Problem Was Really a Cleaning Problem

A call that starts as a pest problem often ends as a cleaning problem - grease and food debris in the extract system and behind equipment, giving pests the warmth, food and shelter they need. Remove that, and the activity usually stops.

The call came in as a pest problem. It turned out to be a cleaning problem wearing a disguise - and once you have seen that pattern a few times, you start to spot it everywhere.

A kitchen manager rings the pest contractor because there are droppings behind the range and something scuttling near the extract canopy at night. Traps go down, bait boxes appear, an invoice follows. A fortnight later the same signs are back. The pests were never the root cause. They were a symptom - the visible end of a warm, sheltered, food-rich environment that a cleaning schedule was supposed to remove and did not. When the harbourage stays, the pests keep coming back, no matter how good your pest contractor is.

What does a pest problem actually tell you about your cleaning?

Pests are not random. They are drawn to three things - food, warmth and shelter - and a poorly cleaned commercial kitchen offers all three in one place. Fats, oils and grease that settle on canopies, filters and the inside of extract ductwork are a food source in their own right, and they cling to fibrous debris that houseflies and cockroaches feed and breed in. Left alone, a greasy extract system becomes a warm, dark, undisturbed corridor running the length of your building - close to ideal harbourage.

This is why an infestation so often points straight back at a cleaning gap rather than a sealing gap. The Building Engineering Services Association sets the benchmark for cleaning kitchen extract systems in its code of practice, TR19® Grease, first issued in 2019. It is blunt on the point: where cleaning to that specification is not carried out, FOG deposits and fibrous particles accumulate and produce a warm greasy environment for bacteria and cockroaches to breed. In other words, the standard that exists to control fire risk is describing the exact conditions that also feed a pest problem.

So when you find droppings, smear marks or dead insects, it is worth asking a different question before you reach for the phone. Not "how do I kill these?" but "what have they found here that they like?" Nine times out of ten the honest answer is grease and food debris in a spot the cleaning rota either skips or reaches too rarely - behind heavy equipment, inside the canopy, up in the ducting, in the bin store, under the floor gullies.

Why does the extract system keep getting missed?

Front-of-kitchen surfaces get wiped every shift. The places pests actually settle are the ones nobody sees. The inside of the extract canopy, the plenum behind the filters, the vertical riser and the horizontal duct runs are out of sight and out of the daily routine, so grease builds there quietly for months. That is precisely where a rodent or an insect will choose to live, because it is warm, sheltered and undisturbed - and it is the hardest part of the kitchen for an untrained hand to reach.

If pests keep returning to the same corner of your kitchen, a thorough commercial kitchen deep clean that reaches behind and inside the equipment usually removes the reason they came.

The daily bins-and-benches routine your team runs is essential, but it is not the same job as a deep clean. A deep clean strips grease and food residue from the areas that day-to-day cleaning cannot touch: under and behind fixed equipment, the full extract canopy and filters, wall and ceiling voids near the cooking line, drainage gullies and the bin and dry-store areas that are so often left off the schedule entirely. Remove the food and the shelter from those places and you have removed the invitation. Baiting without doing this is like emptying a boat with a bucket while the hole stays open.

There is a seasonal edge to this too. UK commercial sites report noticeably higher pest activity in autumn - some pest controllers put it at 20 to 30 percent above the summer baseline - as cooler weather drives rodents and insects indoors looking for exactly the warmth and food a dirty kitchen provides. A deep clean going into that period does more to protect you than any amount of reactive treatment once they are already in.

How does an inspector read the same evidence?

Here is the part that turns a nuisance into a business risk. When an Environmental Health Officer inspects, they are scoring three separate things, and active pest evidence damages all three at once. Under the Food Hygiene Rating Scheme an inspector rates your hygiene procedures, the structural condition of the premises, and their confidence in how you manage the place. Signs of pests read as a hygiene failure, a structural failure if there are gaps and harbourage, and a management failure because your monitoring should have caught it first.

That triple hit is why a pest sighting drags a rating down so hard. Ratings run from 0, meaning urgent improvement is needed, to 5, meaning very good. Confidence in management is scored in bands of 0, 5, 10, 20 or 30 points, and a business that cannot show it saw a problem coming loses heavily there. In serious cases - an imminent risk to public health - an officer can issue an Emergency Prohibition Notice and close the kitchen on the spot. A recurring pest problem you have been treating but not solving is exactly the kind of thing that reads badly on the day.

The flip side is genuinely encouraging. Because pest control sits inside your food safety management system as a prerequisite programme, a clean extract system and a documented deep-clean schedule are evidence in your favour on all three fronts. They show the structure is sound, the hygiene is controlled, and management is on top of things. Understanding how the link between cleaning, pest control and your rating works lets you turn a defensive scramble into a straightforward, provable routine - and it is the same paperwork that shows an EHO you take HACCP seriously in practice rather than just on the wall.

What should you actually do about it?

Treat the pattern, not just the pest. If activity keeps returning to one area, start by looking at what the cleaning schedule reaches there - and, just as important, what it does not. The pest contractor and the cleaning contractor are answering two halves of the same question, and they work best together: one removes the animals, the other removes the reason they came.

A practical order of play looks like this:

  1. Book a deep clean that includes the extract canopy, filters and the ductwork itself to TR19® Grease, not just the surfaces you can see.
  2. Add the usually-forgotten zones - behind fixed equipment, floor gullies, bin stores and dry stores - to a written schedule with dates and sign-off.
  3. Keep the certificates and photographs. They are your evidence for insurers, for your food safety management system and for the next inspection.
  4. Only then judge whether you still have a pest problem. Very often, once the harbourage is gone, the activity simply stops.
