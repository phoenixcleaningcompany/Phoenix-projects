Bain-Maries and Hot Holding: Choosing Kit That Holds Temperature

Hot holding lives or dies on one number - 63°C - and the kit you pick decides how easily you hit it. Here is how wet and dry heat bain-maries compare, and which suits your service.

A bain-marie has one job - keep cooked food at or above 63°C all the way through service - and the way it delivers that heat shapes everything from food quality to your cleaning rota and your energy bill.

Under the Food Safety and Hygiene (England) Regulations 2013, food kept hot for service must be held at 63°C or above. The equivalent rules apply across Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, so wherever you trade the threshold is the same. There is one narrow allowance - food may sit below 63°C for a single period of up to two hours for display or service, after which it must be reheated to 63°C or thrown away - but that is a one-off concession, not a working method. Your hot holding kit needs to sit comfortably above the line for the whole of service, not scrape it.

Most decisions come down to a single fork in the road: wet heat or dry heat. Both are called bain-maries, both take standard Gastronorm pans, and both will pass an inspection if you run them properly. They behave very differently in a real kitchen, though - on warm-up, on the food itself, and on how much of your time they swallow at the end of the night. Here is how they stack up, in plain terms, so you can match the kit to your menu rather than the other way round.

Option A

Wet heat bain-maries

A wet heat unit is the traditional design - a water-filled well beneath the pans, heated by an element in the base. The water sits at roughly 80°C to 85°C and surrounds each Gastronorm pan in gentle, humid heat, a bit like a sauna for the food. That soft, even warmth is why carveries, buffets and pub kitchens have leaned on wet heat for decades.

Pros

  • Gentle, even heat that will not scorch or dry the surface of the food - the best choice for gravies, sauces, custards and moist dishes that catch or curdle over a direct element.
  • Humidity keeps mashed potato, vegetables and casseroles looking fresh and glossy for hours rather than crusting at the edges.
  • No hot spots - the water bath spreads heat right across the well, so a pan of soup holds the same temperature front to back.
  • Forgiving of thin or delicate items that would suffer against a dry element.

Cons

  • Slow to reach service temperature - a wet well can take up to 45 minutes to stabilise, so it needs switching on well before doors open.
  • The water is a hygiene job in its own right. It must be drained, the well cleaned with a food-safe detergent and refilled, and anything that drops in during service means an immediate change and deep clean.
  • Limescale is the quiet killer, especially in hard-water areas. Scale forms on the element and the tank base, and just 1.5mm of build-up can cut heat efficiency by around 12% - meaning the unit works harder and struggles to hold temperature.
  • Heating and holding a body of water uses more energy than warming air, and topping up evaporated water is a running chore.

Option B

Dry heat bain-maries

A dry heat unit skips the water entirely. Electric elements warm the air and metal beneath the pans, bringing the Gastronorm containers up to temperature directly. It is the quicker, simpler design mechanically, and it has become the default for operators who want speed and a lighter cleaning load - buffets holding meats, egg dishes, roast potatoes and other robust items that do not mind a firmer heat.

Pros

  • Fast to service temperature - a dry unit is typically ready in around 15 minutes, a real advantage for split shifts or a kitchen that fires up mid-morning.
  • No water to boil and hold, so running costs are generally lower and there is no evaporation to top up.
  • Far simpler to clean down - wipe out the well, no draining, no descaling of a water tank, which trims minutes off every close.
  • No limescale in the well, so performance stays steadier over time in hard-water areas.

Cons

  • The heat is firmer and less even, so delicate sauces, gravies and custards can catch, skin over or split without careful attention.
  • More prone to hot spots directly above the elements, which can leave one corner of a pan hotter than another.
  • Moist dishes dry out and crust faster than they would in the humid air of a wet well, shortening how good they look on a long buffet.
  • You often end up half-filling pans with a little water anyway for wetter items, which chips away at the simplicity.
Whichever heat you pick, the meter still matters - see our guide on reducing the energy cost of holding food hot before you commit to a unit.

What actually decides it

Menu is the honest deciding factor. If your service leans on sauces, gravies, soups and soft, moist dishes, wet heat protects quality in a way dry heat cannot, and the extra cleaning is simply the price of that. If you are holding roasted meats, potatoes and firmer items and you value a fast start and a quick close, dry heat earns its keep. Plenty of kitchens run both - or a hot cupboard with a bain-marie top, which pairs a dry heated cabinet below for plates and plated meals with wet or dry wells above for gravy and sides. Modern hot cupboards are notably frugal, some costing only a few pence an hour to run, and fan-assisted models can be significantly more efficient than older kit, which matters for both your bill and your CO₂ footprint.

Whatever you choose, the number on the probe is what the inspector checks, not the badge on the unit. Best practice is to probe the centre of the food - the coolest point - and record it at least every two hours through service. That is a habit no bain-marie can do for you, and it is worth knowing exactly what an environmental health officer wants to see written down. A well-specified unit only makes those readings easy to keep above the line.

One point that gets overlooked when kit is chosen: hot holding equipment is a food-contact surface that lives at bacteria-friendly warmth all day, so hygiene is not optional. A wet well left uncleaned harbours Listeria in gaskets and joints; a scaled element quietly loses efficiency until the unit can no longer hold 63°C. Daily draining and cleaning, a weekly deep clean of every insert and removable part, and regular descaling in hard-water areas are what keep a bain-marie both safe and effective. Use mild, food-safe cleaners and never abrasives on the polished stainless steel. Questions

Frequently asked questions

What temperature should a bain-marie hold food at in the UK?

Food must be kept at 63°C or above, which is the legal minimum for hot holding under the Food Safety and Hygiene (England) Regulations 2013 and the equivalent rules elsewhere in the UK. To achieve that, a wet heat bain-marie usually runs its water at around 80°C to 85°C. Always probe the centre of the food, not the water or the air, because the centre is the coolest point.

How long can food safely stay in a bain-marie?

As long as it is held at or above 63°C, food can be kept for service, though quality drops the longer it sits. There is a single legal allowance to keep food below 63°C for up to two hours for display or service, after which it must be reheated to 63°C or thrown away. That two-hour window can only be used once for any given batch, so it is a fallback, not a routine.

Is a wet or dry heat bain-marie better?

It depends on your menu. Wet heat gives gentle, humid warmth that protects sauces, gravies and moist dishes, but it is slow to warm up and needs the water drained, cleaned and descaled. Dry heat is faster to reach temperature and much easier to clean down, but its firmer heat can dry out delicate items, so it suits robust foods like roast meats and potatoes.

How often should a bain-marie be cleaned and descaled?

Drain and clean the well daily, changing the water whenever anything drops in during service, and deep clean every insert and removable part at least weekly. Descaling frequency depends on water hardness - weekly in hard-water areas and monthly in soft-water areas - because even 1.5mm of limescale can cut heat efficiency by around 12%. Left unchecked, scale can stop a unit holding 63°C at all.
