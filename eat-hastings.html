Where to Eat in Hastings

Quick Answer

For a fast answer: Maggie's Fish and Chips on Rock-a-Nore Road for fish landed metres away, Hanushka Coffee House on George Street for coffee and cake among floor-to-ceiling books, Webbe's Rock-a-Nore for Paul Webbe's sustainably sourced seafood restaurant opposite the net huts, and The Crown on All Saints Street for the award-winning Old Town freehouse. Four moods, one ancient fishing town.

There is far more to eat in Hastings than any one page could hold, so this guide keeps it honest: one real, independent standout per category - somewhere to grab a quick bite, a proper coffee, a meal out and a pint - chosen on merit.

The Four Picks

Takeaway

Maggie's Fish and Chips

8-9 Rock-a-Nore Road, the Stade

Maggie's sits directly above the fish market on Rock-a-Nore Road, on the shingle of the Stade where the boats land. Margaret Banfield opened it decades ago and when she retired sold the business to the team that runs it today; what has not changed is the supply chain. Cod and haddock come straight from the market floor below, meaning the fish in the batter was swimming off Hastings that morning. Eat in or take out, the portions are generous, the batter is light and crisp, and the view across the shingle to the net huts is hard to beat. Locals also order the huss (lesser-spotted dogfish), a Hastings-fleet staple you rarely see inland. Rated in the top fifty restaurants in Hastings on TripAdvisor and still pulling queues in high season.

Hanushka Coffee House

28 George Street, Hastings Old Town

George Street is the social spine of Hastings Old Town and Hanushka sits at its heart, a coffee house unlike most others. Every wall from floor to ceiling is lined with secondhand books of every genre, giving the room the feel of a library that also happens to make excellent coffee. Oat-milk lattes, single-origin espresso, Portuguese custard tarts and a changing selection of cakes draw a loyal local crowd and day-trippers looking for something better than a chain. Window seats look out onto one of the Old Town's busiest pedestrian streets, making it the ideal perch for watching Hastings life pass by. Rated 4.5 on Restaurant Guru with nearly 1,700 reviews and open seven days a week from 9am to 6pm.

Webbe's Rock-a-Nore

1 Rock-a-Nore Road, opposite the net huts

Paul Webbe opened this seafood restaurant in 2009 opposite the iconic black-painted net huts of the Stade, and it has become one of the south coast's most-respected fish restaurants. Webbe sources exclusively from sustainable, MSC-certified fisheries, and the menu follows the catch: grilled Dover sole with samphire and winkle sauce, steamed flounder with mussel and cider sauce, gratinated native lobster, and a cod in beer batter with hand-cut chips for those who want the classic done properly. There is a seasonal outside terrace with views of the boats. The restaurant also runs a fish cookery school. Rated 4.8 out of 5 on Restaurant Guru with over 3,000 reviews and bookable on OpenTable. Book ahead; it fills quickly.

The Crown

64-66 All Saints Street, Hastings Old Town

There has been a Crown Inn on All Saints Street since at least the eighteenth century, but the pub that stands today was rescued in July 2014 by Tess and Andrew Swan, who transformed a building that had been dark for years into one of the best-run independents on the coast. The result is a genuine freehouse: Sussex and Kent producers supply the kitchen, the local fishing fleet supplies the fish, and the beer list is kept with care. In 2017 it won The Observer's Best Place to Drink in the UK, and in 2018 was named Parliamentary Pub of the Year. The menu runs to beer-battered fish finger sandwiches, seasonal meat and veg, and dishes that change as produce does. The kitchen runs 12 to 4 and 5 to 9, 364 days a year.

Hastings's Food Scene

Hastings eats around its ancient fishing heart. The Stade on the shingle beach at the eastern end of the Old Town is home to Europe's largest fleet of beach-launched fishing boats, and the black-tarred net huts that stand behind the boats have stored sails and gear since the seventeenth century. Rock-a-Nore Road, which runs along the seafront past the net huts, is where the fish comes ashore, and where the town's most characterful seafood restaurants and chip shops have gathered.

A short walk inland, George Street and the Old Town High Street form the social and culinary core of the historic quarter: independent coffee houses, wine bars, delis and small restaurants filling the ground floors of medieval and Georgian buildings. The area has attracted a wave of creative independents drawn by low rents and a strong local community, making it one of the most genuinely characterful eating streets in the south-east. All Saints Street, running parallel to the High Street and leading up towards the clifftop country park, carries the best of the pub culture.

The town's signature ingredient is fresh fish, particularly the varieties that come off Hastings-fleet boats: Dover sole, plaice, huss, mackerel and cuttlefish are all local staples. Cuttlefish, often turned into ink pasta or grilled whole, is the dish that most marks Hastings out from other seaside towns. The fishing fleet lands daily year-round, and the restaurants and chip shops that sit metres from the beach offer a supply chain that few inland cities can match.

Behind the Pass: The Kitchens That Keep Hastings Fed

Fryers, grills and pass after pass through a busy season put a commercial kitchen's extraction system - canopy, baffle filters, plenum, ductwork and fan - under real strain, drawing grease-laden vapour out of the building. Hastings kitchens run on the freshest possible fish: landed daily from the Stade's beach-launched fleet, the catch goes straight into fryers, grills and smokers, and a seaside fryer running flat-out through a summer service pushes a heavy load of grease-laden vapour into its canopy every hour.

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for a premises must assess and manage fire risk, and a grease-laden duct is one of the most serious risks in any catering building. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) reinforce the same duty of care, while an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) or a buildings insurer can ask to see documented proof the extraction system has been professionally cleaned.

What is TR19 Grease?

TR19 Grease is the UK industry standard for cleaning ductwork and extraction systems in commercial kitchens, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It defines how grease deposits are measured, the maximum thickness allowed before cleaning is due, and the post-clean certification a kitchen should hold - the benchmark insurers, fire assessors and EHOs look for.

A seaside kitchen earns its year in a few hot months. A clean extraction system is what keeps it trading through every one of them.

Industry Insight

The Cleaning Frequency Most Operators Get Wrong

Here is something many venue owners do not realise: under TR19 Grease, cleaning frequency is set by how many hours the kitchen actually cooks, not by how the canopy looks. A heavy-use kitchen running 12 to 16 hours a day should be cleaned roughly every three months. Moderate use of 6 to 12 hours points to every six months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours to every twelve.

The catch is that baffle filters can look clean while the plenum, the horizontal duct runs and the fan housing carry a heavy grease load that throttles airflow and lowers extract efficiency. A fire in that concealed ductwork spreads fast. That is why a dated TR19 certificate, kept in the fire logbook, is the document an insurer expects to see - and its absence at the point of a claim is a common reason cover is refused.

Planning Your Visit

Almost all of this guide sits within walking distance in the Old Town. Maggie's and Webbe's both stand on Rock-a-Nore Road at the eastern end of the seafront, a ten-minute walk from the centre; Hanushka is on George Street in the middle of the Old Town; and The Crown is on All Saints Street, five minutes from George Street on foot. Hastings station is about a mile from the Old Town, with taxis or the seafront walk both easy options. Parking is available in the Stade car park right next to Rock-a-Nore.

The day flows naturally from east to west. Start with fish and chips at Maggie's on the Stade, walk the seafront past the net huts to Webbe's if you want a more formal seafood lunch, continue up to George Street for coffee and cake at Hanushka in the afternoon, and end the evening at The Crown on All Saints Street. Book Webbe's and The Crown in advance, especially on summer weekends. Webbe's terrace seats are first come, first served.

  • Walk Rock-a-Nore Road past the net huts before eating - the Stade sets the scene
  • Ask Maggie's for the huss if it is on - a local Hastings-fleet catch rarely found elsewhere
  • Book Webbe's Rock-a-Nore ahead; the terrace fills fast on fine days
  • Settle in at Hanushka on George Street with a coffee and browse the books
  • Book The Crown for dinner - kitchen closes at 9pm, open 364 days a year

What to Order and How to Do the Day

Order with intent. At Maggie's, cod or haddock in light batter with chips, or the huss if available - a true Hastings fleet catch. At Hanushka, a flat white or oat-milk latte and a Portuguese custard tart. At Webbe's, follow the catch of the day, but the Dover sole with samphire and the native lobster are perennial signatures. At The Crown, the beer-battered fish finger sandwich is the house classic, or ask what local fish the kitchen has in that week.

Frequently Asked Questions

Hastings Food and Drink FAQ

Where can I eat the freshest fish in Hastings?

Maggie's Fish and Chips on Rock-a-Nore Road sits directly above the fish market; the cod and haddock come from the market floor below the restaurant and the Stade fishing fleet is metres away. Webbe's Rock-a-Nore, also on Rock-a-Nore Road, offers a restaurant version of the same supply chain.

What is the local signature dish in Hastings?

Fresh fish, particularly varieties from the Hastings beach-launched fleet, is the town signature. Huss (lesser-spotted dogfish) is a local staple found on the boats and in the chip shops. Cuttlefish, often grilled or turned into ink pasta, marks Hastings out from other coastal towns.

Which Hastings pub has won national awards?

The Crown on All Saints Street in Hastings Old Town, a family-run freehouse reopened in 2014, won The Observer's Best Place to Drink in the UK in 2017 and was named Parliamentary Pub of the Year in 2018. It is also listed in The Good Food Guide's Top 100 Pubs and Harden's.

Is Webbe's Rock-a-Nore a good restaurant for seafood?

Yes. Chef Paul Webbe opened Webbe's Rock-a-Nore in 2009 opposite the Stade net huts, and it has become one of the south coast's best-regarded fish restaurants. It holds MSC sustainable seafood certification and is rated 4.8 on Restaurant Guru with over 3,000 reviews. Book ahead.

What is the Stade in Hastings?

The Stade is the shingle beach at the eastern end of Hastings Old Town, home to Europe's largest fleet of beach-launched fishing boats. The distinctive black-tarred net huts behind the boats are seventeenth-century structures once used to store sails and nets. Rock-a-Nore Road runs along the beach past the huts, with fish restaurants and the local fish market alongside.

Are these Hastings food spots easy to reach without a car?

Yes. Maggie's and Webbe's are on Rock-a-Nore Road at the eastern end of the seafront, a level walk from the Old Town. Hanushka is on George Street in the heart of the Old Town, and The Crown is on All Saints Street five minutes further. Hastings station is about a mile away, with a taxi or scenic seafront walk to reach the Old Town.
