Where to Eat in Lincoln

Quick Answer

For a fast answer: Elite on the Bail on Bailgate for award-winning Lincolnshire haddock in the Cathedral Quarter, Stokes High Bridge Cafe for a 120-year-old roaster in a Tudor building over the River Witham, The Jews House Restaurant on The Strait for chef Gavin Aitkenhead's modern tasting menus in a Norman stone house, and The Strugglers Inn on Westgate for nine hand-pulled ales beneath Lincoln Castle. Four moods, one cobbled uphill city.

There is far more to eat in Lincoln than any one page could hold, so this guide keeps it honest: one real, independent standout per category - somewhere to grab a quick bite, a proper coffee, a meal out and a pint - chosen on merit.

The Four Picks

Takeaway/Casual

Elite on the Bail

21-22 Bailgate, Cathedral Quarter

The Elite Fish and Chip Company began in Ruskington in 1992 and won the National Fish and Chip of the Year Award that same year. The family business grew to a large Lincoln restaurant on Tritton Road before opening this fully licensed Bailgate branch in 2019, placing their sustainably sourced fish within a short walk of the Cathedral and Lincoln Castle. The menu runs from fresh haddock and cod in a light batter to grilled fish, prawn cocktail and Lincolnshire-sourced sides, and a full gluten-free menu is always available. It earned a Food Hygiene Rating of 5 (inspected June 2026) and reviews consistently praise the quality and size of the fish. Open Tuesday to Sunday from 11:30.

Stokes High Bridge Cafe

207 High Street, High Bridge

Stokes Tea and Coffee traces its story to 1892, when Robert William Stokes arrived in Lincoln as a young grocer. By 1902 he was roasting his own blends, and the award-winning beans and service spread his name across the city. The family purchased the building on Lincoln's High Bridge shortly afterwards and opened the flagship cafe in 1937. The High Bridge is the only medieval bridge in England still lined with buildings, and the structure dates to 1540 in its current form. Stokes remains a fourth-generation family roastery: Nick Peel, great-grandson of the founder, runs the business today from its Lawn roastery, while the High Bridge cafe pours single-origin espresso and over thirty loose-leaf teas in rooms of winding staircases and low beams. Open seven days a week.

The Jews House Restaurant

15 The Strait, below Steep Hill

The building at 15 The Strait is one of the oldest surviving domestic stone houses in England, built around 1170 in the Norman period and later associated with Lincoln's medieval Jewish community. It has been a restaurant since about 1973, and the current chapter is overseen by chef-patron Gavin Aitkenhead and front-of-house director Samantha Tomkins. The cooking is modern British: seasonal British produce treated with precision and imagination across a three-course menu (Wednesday to Thursday from £88), a four-course and a six-course tasting menu. The low-beamed dining room holds perhaps thirty covers and books out quickly. Recent reviews from January 2026 confirm the kitchen is firing well, with one reviewer noting the quality has risen alongside the prices. Book well ahead; the restaurant is open Wednesday to Saturday.

Pub

The Strugglers Inn

83 Westgate, beneath Lincoln Castle

The Strugglers has stood on Westgate since 1841, taking its name from the public hangings once carried out at the castle next door, and the pub sign still shows a figure being led to the gallows. The atmosphere today is more cheerful: a traditional two-roomed local with nine hand-pulled cask ales on rotation, pump clips from past brews covering the walls, two fireplaces and a sheltered garden. It is listed in the CAMRA Good Beer Guide, has won Lincoln CAMRA Pub of the Year multiple times including 2023 and 2025, and was the branch entry for the overall CAMRA Lincolnshire competition in 2026. The ever-changing range regularly features stouts, porters, strong milds and barley wines that few other Lincoln pubs bother to stock. Under 18s are not admitted.

Lincoln's Food Scene

Lincoln divides naturally into uphill and downhill, and so does its food. The lower city sits on the flat Roman grid around the High Street and the River Witham, where Stokes Coffee roasted its first beans on the Tudor-fronted High Bridge. From there, The Strait and Steep Hill climb a gradient the Ordnance Survey rates the fourth steepest in England, lined with independent cafes, bakeries and restaurants as they go.

At the top, the Cathedral Quarter around Bailgate and the castle forms Lincoln's densest patch of independent food and drink. Bailgate runs between Castle Square and Newport Arch, the only Roman arch in Britain still carrying motor traffic, and the street is lined with the kind of independent businesses that make a food quarter work. The Lincoln Sausage Festival brings thousands up here each autumn to celebrate the county's most famous product: a coarse-ground pork sausage seasoned with sage, made by local butchers who have been curing and smoking Lincolnshire pork for generations.

Alongside the Lincolnshire sausage, the county's other signature is plum bread, a dense, fruit-packed tea loaf first made commercially in Alford in 1901 and now found in every Lincoln bakery, best served with butter and a wedge of local cheese. Between the Roman walls and the Norman castle, the eating is rooted in the landscape: pork, fish from the Lincolnshire coast, and a coffee culture that, thanks to Stokes, predates the national speciality wave by about a century.

Behind the Pass: The Kitchens That Keep Lincoln Fed

Every kitchen in this guide, however small, runs an extraction system - canopy, baffle filters, plenum, ductwork and fan - that pulls heat, smoke and grease-laden air off the cookline. Lincoln's compact uphill kitchens work at full stretch all week feeding visitors who have climbed Steep Hill to the Cathedral and castle, and the grease load from busy fryers, breakfast griddles and evening services flows through ducting inside medieval and Georgian buildings where clean extraction is both a legal duty and a fire-safety necessity.

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for a premises must assess and manage fire risk, and a grease-laden duct is one of the most serious risks in any catering building. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) reinforce the same duty of care, while an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) or a buildings insurer can ask to see documented proof the extraction system has been professionally cleaned.

What is TR19 Grease?

TR19 Grease is the UK industry standard for cleaning ductwork and extraction systems in commercial kitchens, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It defines how grease deposits are measured, the maximum thickness allowed before cleaning is due, and the post-clean certification a kitchen should hold - the benchmark insurers, fire assessors and EHOs look for.

A small kitchen is no less of a fire risk than a big one. A clean, certified extraction system is what keeps it safe and insured.

Industry Insight

The Cleaning Frequency Most Operators Get Wrong

Here is something many venue owners do not realise: under TR19 Grease, cleaning frequency is set by how many hours the kitchen actually cooks, not by how the canopy looks. A heavy-use kitchen running 12 to 16 hours a day should be cleaned roughly every three months. Moderate use of 6 to 12 hours points to every six months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours to every twelve.

The catch is that baffle filters can look clean while the plenum, the horizontal duct runs and the fan housing carry a heavy grease load that throttles airflow and lowers extract efficiency. A fire in that concealed ductwork spreads fast. That is why a dated TR19 certificate, kept in the fire logbook, is the document an insurer expects to see - and its absence at the point of a claim is a common reason cover is refused.

Planning Your Visit

The geography is compact and very walkable from the uphill area. The Strugglers Inn and Elite on the Bail are both within a few minutes of Lincoln Castle, and The Jews House sits halfway up Steep Hill on The Strait. The one exception is Stokes High Bridge Cafe, which is at the bottom of the hill in the lower city, a fifteen-minute walk down or a short bus ride.

Done as a day: coffee at Stokes High Bridge in the morning, a walk up Steep Hill to browse the independent shops, fish and chips at Elite on the Bail in the Cathedral Quarter, a booking at The Jews House for the evening and a nightcap pint of cask ale at The Strugglers as the castle closes. The hill is steep but the reward at the top is one of the finest urban views in England.

  • Start at Stokes High Bridge Cafe and look down at the River Witham from the medieval bridge
  • Book The Jews House Restaurant well ahead; it is small and fills quickly on weekends
  • Climb Steep Hill to the Cathedral Quarter, pausing at the independent shops on The Strait
  • Pick up a Lincolnshire sausage roll or a slice of plum bread from a Bailgate bakery
  • The Strugglers Inn is adults-only; no under-18s are admitted

What to Order and How to Do the Day

Order with intent. At Elite on the Bail, fresh haddock in light batter with proper chips, or the gluten-free version if needed. At Stokes High Bridge, ask which single-origin is on the espresso and pair it with a scone and their Flo's Mix loose-leaf blend. At The Jews House, lean into the seasonal tasting menu and let chef Aitkenhead choose the direction. At The Strugglers, ask the bar what is unusual on the handpulls today; they regularly pour styles most pubs never stock.

Frequently Asked Questions

Lincoln Food and Drink FAQ

Where is the best fish and chips in Lincoln?

Elite on the Bail at 21-22 Bailgate is a fully licensed restaurant and takeaway from the Elite Fish and Chip Company, which has been frying since 1992. It uses sustainably sourced fish, holds a 5-star food hygiene rating and offers a full gluten-free menu.

What is the oldest building used as a restaurant in Lincoln?

The Jews House Restaurant on The Strait occupies a Norman stone house built around 1170, making it one of the oldest domestic buildings in England. Chef Gavin Aitkenhead has run a modern British tasting-menu restaurant there since the current incarnation.

Which is Lincoln's best coffee shop?

Stokes High Bridge Cafe at 207 High Street has been roasting and serving coffee in a Tudor building on the only medieval bridge in England with houses on it since 1937. The family business, now in its fourth generation, sources single-origin beans and stocks over thirty loose-leaf teas.

What is the Lincolnshire sausage?

The Lincolnshire sausage is a coarse-ground pork sausage seasoned predominantly with sage, made by local butchers across the county. Lincoln holds an annual Sausage Festival in the Cathedral Quarter each autumn celebrating this and other local produce.

Which Lincoln pub has the best real ale?

The Strugglers Inn at 83 Westgate, a two-roomed pub established in 1841 beneath Lincoln Castle, keeps nine rotating hand-pulled cask ales including rare styles like stout, porter and barley wine. It has won Lincoln CAMRA Pub of the Year in 2023 and 2025 and appears in the CAMRA Good Beer Guide.

How do you get around Lincoln to eat?

The uphill Cathedral Quarter, where The Jews House, Elite on the Bail and The Strugglers Inn are all located, is walkable in under ten minutes. Stokes High Bridge Cafe is at the bottom of Steep Hill in the lower city, fifteen minutes on foot or a short bus ride. The bus station and train station are both in the lower city.
