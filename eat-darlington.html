Where to Eat in Darlington

Quick Answer

For a fast answer: Marketplace Fish N Chips inside the 1863 Covered Market for beef-dripping battered fish, Robineau on West Auckland Road for a Loire-trained patissier's afternoon tea, The Bay Horse at Hurworth-on-Tees for two-AA-Rosette modern British cooking in a 15th-century coaching inn, and Number Twenty 2 on Coniscliffe Road for sixteen handpumps and Darlington's own Village Brewer ales. Four moods, one compact railway town.

There is far more to eat in Darlington than any one page could hold, so this guide keeps it honest: one real, independent standout per category - somewhere to grab a quick bite, a proper coffee, a meal out and a pint - chosen on merit.

The Four Picks

Takeaway/Casual

Marketplace Fish N Chips

Covered Market, East Row, town centre

Darlington's Victorian Covered Market has traded since 1863, and the Marketplace Fish N Chips stall occupies the original fishmonger's counter inside the hall. Run by Adam Gilmore, it fries exclusively in beef dripping - the traditional north-east method that gives the batter its amber crust and clean, rich flavour. The market's stone-and-ironwork interior makes it one of the more atmospheric places to eat chips in the region. Portions are generous and cooked fresh to order; the wet-fish counter alongside lets you take home the day's catch as well. It is open market hours, Monday to Saturday, and is the quickest honest meal in the town centre.

Robineau Cafe & Patisserie

27 West Auckland Road, Cockerton village

Two young pastry chefs - Fred, from the Loire valley, and Jane, from Barnard Castle - met in the kitchens of the Connaught Hotel in London and opened Robineau in Cockerton in 2007. The result is something genuinely unusual in a north-eastern market town: a full French-trained patisserie producing handmade eclairs, tarts and chocolates alongside proper English fruit scones with vanilla cream. The afternoon tea is the thing to book ahead; the shop also stocks hand-crafted chocolate bars and biscuits to take home. Tables are available Wednesday to Saturday from 09:30 for breakfast, lunch or afternoon tea, and the Cockerton setting - a quiet Victorian suburb west of the centre - is a step removed from the high street.

Known for

Restaurant

The Bay Horse at Hurworth

45 The Green, Hurworth-on-Tees, three miles south

Set in a 15th-century coaching inn on the village green at Hurworth-on-Tees, The Bay Horse is Darlington's destination restaurant. Chef-patron Marcus Bennett has held a Michelin Guide listing since the pub's transformation and the kitchen was upgraded to two AA Rosettes in January 2026 - recognition for cooking that takes the finest local and seasonal produce seriously without losing the relaxed pub feel. The dining room pairs original stone and beams with modern decor; private rooms and a walled garden are bookable for occasions. The seasonal menu changes regularly around British classics executed with precision: expect dry-aged beef, fish from the North Sea coast and puddings worth staying for. Book well ahead, especially weekends.

Pub

Number Twenty 2

22 Coniscliffe Road, town centre

Darlington is widely regarded as a real-ale destination, and Number Twenty 2 is its most celebrated house. The town-centre pub on Coniscliffe Road pours from up to sixteen handpumps - cask ales, two real ciders and a stout or porter as constants, with guest lines rotating from across the north - and backs them with ten draught European beers. The pub produces its own Village Brewer range (including Zetland Pale, commissioned from Hambleton) and runs a nano-distillery to the rear making gin, rum and vodka. The interior is light and airy with huge curved windows and stained-glass panels, a far cry from the usual ale-house. A long-standing multiple CAMRA award winner, it is the kind of place serious beer drinkers plan a trip to Darlington around.

Darlington's Food Scene

Darlington's food geography centres on the town centre and its Victorian infrastructure. The Covered Market, built in 1863 and one of the finest surviving examples in the north-east, anchors the daily food trade with its fishmonger's slab, butchers and a growing street-food hall. Skinnergate and the surrounding lanes hold the town's independent restaurants, from Italian to Bangladeshi, clustered within easy walking distance of the Market Place.

The Quaker heritage that shaped the town - Edward Pease and the Society of Friends backed the world's first passenger railway in 1825 and built many of Darlington's Victorian civic buildings - left a tradition of sober industry rather than grand dining. The independent food scene that has grown up in its wake is practical and unpretentious: good fish and chips, proper cask ale, serious coffee and a French-trained patissier who chose a quiet Victorian suburb over a city-centre site.

The best of Darlington's food sits on the fringes: the Bay Horse gastropub is three miles south in the river-meadow village of Hurworth-on-Tees, and Robineau's patisserie is out in Cockerton to the west. The town centre holds the market and the real-ale pubs; the surrounding villages hold the destination dining. Between them they make a coherent food day out of a town that rarely gets the credit it deserves.

Behind the Pass: The Kitchens That Keep Darlington Fed

Every kitchen in this guide, however small, runs an extraction system - canopy, baffle filters, plenum, ductwork and fan - that pulls heat, smoke and grease-laden air off the cookline. Darlington's market kitchens and gastropubs have been running hard since the Victorian era, and a fryer searing fish in beef dripping all day or a pub kitchen firing Saturday covers puts a heavy, sustained load of grease-laden vapour into canopies and ductwork.

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for a premises must assess and manage fire risk, and a grease-laden duct is one of the most serious risks in any catering building. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) reinforce the same duty of care, while an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) or a buildings insurer can ask to see documented proof the extraction system has been professionally cleaned.

What is TR19 Grease?

TR19 Grease is the UK industry standard for cleaning ductwork and extraction systems in commercial kitchens, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It defines how grease deposits are measured, the maximum thickness allowed before cleaning is due, and the post-clean certification a kitchen should hold - the benchmark insurers, fire assessors and EHOs look for.

A small kitchen is no less of a fire risk than a big one. A clean, certified extraction system is what keeps it safe and insured.

Industry Insight

The Cleaning Frequency Most Operators Get Wrong

Here is something many venue owners do not realise: under TR19 Grease, cleaning frequency is set by how many hours the kitchen actually cooks, not by how the canopy looks. A heavy-use kitchen running 12 to 16 hours a day should be cleaned roughly every three months. Moderate use of 6 to 12 hours points to every six months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours to every twelve.

The catch is that baffle filters can look clean while the plenum, the horizontal duct runs and the fan housing carry a heavy grease load that throttles airflow and lowers extract efficiency. A fire in that concealed ductwork spreads fast. That is why a dated TR19 certificate, kept in the fire logbook, is the document an insurer expects to see - and its absence at the point of a claim is a common reason cover is refused.

Planning Your Visit

The town centre sites are compact: the Covered Market, Number Twenty 2 on Coniscliffe Road and the Skinnergate restaurant strip are all within ten minutes on foot. Robineau is a short drive or taxi west into Cockerton. The Bay Horse at Hurworth is three miles south - a pleasant drive along the Tees valley or a short taxi from the station.

Darlington is well served by rail - it sits on the East Coast Main Line and is a direct stop from London King's Cross, Edinburgh and the major northern cities. The railway connection is part of the town's identity: the Locomotion No. 1 replica at the Head of Steam museum is two minutes from the station, and the town has quietly built a food scene worthy of the journey.

  • Visit the Covered Market on a weekday morning for fish and chips at the Marketplace stall
  • Book Robineau ahead for Wednesday to Saturday sittings - afternoon tea fills quickly
  • Reserve The Bay Horse at Hurworth well in advance, especially for weekends
  • Allow an afternoon for Number Twenty 2 - sixteen handpumps rewards a considered approach
  • Use Darlington railway station as your base; the East Coast Main Line stops here direct

What to Order and How to Do the Day

Order with intent. At Marketplace Fish N Chips, the classic battered cod in beef dripping with chips, fresh from the counter. At Robineau, the afternoon tea with French patisserie and fruit scones, or take home a box of their hand-crafted chocolates. At The Bay Horse, follow the seasonal menu and trust the kitchen - the dry-aged beef and the puddings are the anchors. At Number Twenty 2, ask the staff what is new on the guest lines and work through a half-pint of Zetland Pale from the Village Brewer range.

Frequently Asked Questions

Darlington Food and Drink FAQ

What is the best restaurant in Darlington?

The Bay Horse at Hurworth-on-Tees, three miles south of the town centre, holds two AA Rosettes (awarded January 2026) and a Michelin Guide listing. Chef-patron Marcus Bennett serves modern British cooking from seasonal local produce in a restored 15th-century coaching inn on the village green.

Where can I get fish and chips in Darlington town centre?

Marketplace Fish N Chips trades from the original fishmonger's counter inside Darlington's Victorian Covered Market on East Row. They fry exclusively in beef dripping - the traditional north-east method - and are open Monday to Saturday during market hours.

What is Darlington known for historically?

Darlington is the birthplace of the railways: the Stockton and Darlington Railway, the world's first public steam passenger railway, opened on 27 September 1825, conceived by Quaker merchant Edward Pease and built by George Stephenson. The town's Quaker heritage also shaped its Victorian civic buildings and its market.

Where is the best pub for real ale in Darlington?

Number Twenty 2 on Coniscliffe Road is a multiple CAMRA award winner pouring from up to sixteen handpumps, including the house Village Brewer range and rotating guest ales from across the north. It also runs a nano-distillery producing its own gin, rum and vodka.

Is there a good cafe or patisserie in Darlington?

Robineau on West Auckland Road in Cockerton has been making authentic French patisserie, handcrafted chocolates and English afternoon teas since 2007. The founders trained in the pastry kitchen of the Connaught Hotel in London and the quality has remained consistent.

How do I get to Darlington?

Darlington is on the East Coast Main Line with direct trains from London King's Cross, Edinburgh, Newcastle and York. The town centre, Covered Market and Coniscliffe Road pubs are ten minutes on foot from the station. The Bay Horse at Hurworth is three miles south by car or taxi.
