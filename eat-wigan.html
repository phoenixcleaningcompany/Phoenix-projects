Where to Eat in Wigan

Quick Answer

For a fast answer: Whittles Bakery in Pemberton for a freshly baked pie barm from the fourth generation of the Whittle family, Mayhap Coffee in Tyldesley for speciality coffee and pancakes in a restored historic shopfront, Fat Olive on Upper Dicconson Street for family-run Mediterranean cooking in the town centre, and the Swan and Railway Hotel on Wallgate for eight real ales in a Grade II listed Victorian pub restored to its 1898 best. Four moods, one proudly independent town.

There is far more to eat in Wigan than any one page could hold, so this guide keeps it honest: one real, independent standout per category - somewhere to grab a quick bite, a proper coffee, a meal out and a pint - chosen on merit.

The Four Picks

Pie Bakery

Whittles Bakery

1 Tunstall Lane, Pemberton

The Whittle family has been baking on Tunstall Lane in Pemberton for generations. Their great-grandfather started the business in Scholes, and the shop eventually settled on Tunstall Lane where three generations kept it going before a fourteen-year gap. In January 2024 twin sisters Hannah and Grace Whittle, fourth-generation bakers, brought it back to life with the same values: everything homemade, everything fresh, baked from scratch every morning. On opening day customers queued around the corner. The meat and potato, chicken and mushroom, and steak pies are made with proper shortcrust pastry and generous fillings, and the order to make in Wigan is the pie barm, locally known as a Wigan Kebab: a hot pie tucked into a soft barm cake, the town's signature street food since long before the term existed.

Mayhap Coffee

1 Chapel Street, Tyldesley

Mayhap opened on Chapel Street in Tyldesley, in the Metropolitan Borough of Wigan, with a clear mission: to bring the independent speciality coffee experience out of city centres and into the communities that actually need it. The owner was among the first on the Tyldesley high street to restore a historic shopfront, rescuing a previously derelict building with a traditional facade and opening a coffee house on the ground floor that now draws queues from across Greater Manchester. The coffee is taken seriously without the attitude: rotating speciality espresso and filter, loose-leaf teas, and a brunch menu that has built a loyal following on its pancake stacks and French toast specials. Open daily from 8 am, no bookings taken, dog friendly.

Restaurant

Fat Olive

13 Upper Dicconson Street, Wigan town centre

Two minutes' walk from Wigan town centre, Fat Olive is a family-run restaurant and bar on Upper Dicconson Street that takes Mediterranean cooking and grounds it in Lancashire produce. Executive chef Matt Varley spent fifteen years working in France, Spain and the Isle of Man before developing the kitchen's Lancashire-inflected approach: locally sourced ingredients cooked with a southern European sensibility, in a room of bare brickwork and warm modern decor that sits a long way from the high street. The lunch and dinner menus run from small sharing plates and mezze-style starters through grills and pasta to well-made desserts, and the bar holds a solid wine list. It is the kind of independent that rewards a booking on a Friday night, and a reliable weekday lunch stop for the town centre crowd.

Pub

Swan and Railway Hotel

Wallgate, opposite Wigan Wallgate station

Built in 1898 to face Wigan Wallgate station and Grade II listed by Historic England, the Swan and Railway was once described as the worst pub in Wigan. Its 2018-20 restoration reversed that verdict decisively. The Victorian interior has been returned to its original character: a tiled drinking corridor with a mosaic floor, oxblood red leather seating, an elegant front lounge and a majestic bar topped by a stained-glass swan. The pub won a Historic England Conservation Award in 2021 and was named Wigan CAMRA Branch Pub of the Year 2026. Eight handpumps pour a rotating range of real ales, the pub sponsors the annual Wigan Beer Festival, and the World Pie Eating Championship is held nearby on Wallgate each December. Honest food, fine ales and a genuinely remarkable room.

Wigan's Food Scene

Wigan's food identity is rooted in two things: the pie and the pub. The town has been called the pie capital of England, and the nickname is earned. The annual World Pie Eating Championship has been held on Wallgate since 1992, with competitors racing to finish a single 12 cm beef and potato pie. The pie barm, a hot pie served inside a soft barm cake, is the town's signature street food and has been cheerfully nicknamed the Wigan Kebab for decades.

The town centre, grouped around the Market Place, Wallgate and the Grand Arcade, holds the main cluster of independent eating and drinking. The arches under the railway viaduct on Queen Street have become a food and drink destination in their own right, and the canal quarter around Wigan Pier, made famous by George Orwell's 1937 book, is being redeveloped as a cultural and leisure hub. Pemberton and Tyldesley, inner-borough districts a short ride from the centre, hold some of the most interesting independents including a fourth-generation bakery and a speciality coffee house that draws visitors from across Greater Manchester.

Sweet lovers know Wigan as the home of Uncle Joe's Mint Balls, the peppermint sweets made at The Toffee Works by the Santus family since 1898, the same year the Swan and Railway Hotel was built. Beyond the classics, a newer generation of independent cafes, restaurants and bars is building on that tradition, bringing speciality coffee, Mediterranean cooking and seriously kept real ale into a town that has always known how to feed itself.

Behind the Pass: The Kitchens That Keep Wigan Fed

Behind every bar in this guide is a kitchen, and behind every kitchen an extraction system - canopy, baffle filters, plenum, ductwork and fan - drawing grease-laden air off the cookline and out through the roof. Wigan's pubs and pie shops run hot all week: the fryers turning out hundreds of fresh pies from early morning and the busy pub kitchens firing food through lunch and dinner pour a steady load of grease-laden vapour into their canopies every service.

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for a premises must assess and manage fire risk, and a grease-laden duct is one of the most serious risks in any catering building. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) reinforce the same duty of care, while an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) or a buildings insurer can ask to see documented proof the extraction system has been professionally cleaned.

What is TR19 Grease?

TR19 Grease is the UK industry standard for cleaning ductwork and extraction systems in commercial kitchens, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It defines how grease deposits are measured, the maximum thickness allowed before cleaning is due, and the post-clean certification a kitchen should hold - the benchmark insurers, fire assessors and EHOs look for.

A great pub kitchen lives or dies on its food. The extraction system is what keeps it open, insured and safe behind the scenes.

Industry Insight

The Cleaning Frequency Most Operators Get Wrong

Here is something many venue owners do not realise: under TR19 Grease, cleaning frequency is set by how many hours the kitchen actually cooks, not by how the canopy looks. A heavy-use kitchen running 12 to 16 hours a day should be cleaned roughly every three months. Moderate use of 6 to 12 hours points to every six months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours to every twelve.

The catch is that baffle filters can look clean while the plenum, the horizontal duct runs and the fan housing carry a heavy grease load that throttles airflow and lowers extract efficiency. A fire in that concealed ductwork spreads fast. That is why a dated TR19 certificate, kept in the fire logbook, is the document an insurer expects to see - and its absence at the point of a claim is a common reason cover is refused.

Planning Your Visit

Most of the town-centre venues are within easy walking distance of Wigan North Western and Wigan Wallgate stations, which sit minutes apart and connect to Manchester, Liverpool and Preston. The Swan and Railway faces Wallgate station directly; Fat Olive is a short walk uphill into the centre. Pemberton for Whittles Bakery and Tyldesley for Mayhap Coffee are both a short bus or taxi ride, easily paired with a town-centre visit.

Time it right and the day flows naturally: a pie barm from Whittles Bakery in the morning, coffee and brunch at Mayhap in Tyldesley, a Mediterranean lunch or dinner at Fat Olive in the centre, and a pint of well-kept ale in the Swan and Railway's Victorian tiled corridor to finish. The town is more compact than its reputation for pies suggests, and the food scene rewards those who venture past the high street.

  • Head to Whittles Bakery on Tunstall Lane early - the pies sell out by midday
  • Take the bus or a short drive to Tyldesley for brunch at Mayhap Coffee
  • Book Fat Olive on Upper Dicconson Street for an evening meal in town
  • Walk down Wallgate to the Swan and Railway for a pint of real ale
  • Both Wigan stations are minutes apart - trains run to Manchester and Liverpool

What to Order and How to Do the Day

Order with intent. At Whittles Bakery, a hot meat and potato pie inside a barm cake: the pie barm, Wigan's own street food. At Mayhap Coffee, the pancake stack or French toast special of the month, with a rotating speciality espresso. At Fat Olive, share the mezze starters and lean into the grill mains with a glass from the Mediterranean wine list. At the Swan and Railway, ask which of the eight handpump ales is drinking best, find a corner of the tiled corridor, and look up at the Victorian craftsmanship.

Frequently Asked Questions

Wigan Food and Drink FAQ

What is a Wigan Kebab?

A Wigan Kebab is the local name for a pie barm: a freshly baked hot pie served inside a soft barm cake. It has been Wigan's signature street food for generations and is the order to make at any of the town's traditional pie bakeries, including Whittles Bakery on Tunstall Lane in Pemberton.

Where is the World Pie Eating Championship held?

The World Pie Eating Championship has been held annually on Wallgate in Wigan since 1992. Competitors race to eat a single 12 cm beef and potato pie as fast as possible. The 2025 event was held in December at Harry's Bar on Wallgate.

Where can I get the best independent coffee in Wigan?

Mayhap Coffee at 1 Chapel Street in Tyldesley, part of the Metropolitan Borough of Wigan, is one of the most-praised independents in the area: speciality espresso and filter, loose-leaf tea, and a brunch menu famous for its pancake stacks. Open daily from 8 am, no bookings needed.

Which Wigan pub has the best interior?

The Swan and Railway Hotel on Wallgate, built in 1898 and Grade II listed, has one of the finest restored Victorian pub interiors in the North West: a tiled drinking corridor with mosaic floor, oxblood leather seating, and a stained-glass swan above the bar. It won a Historic England Conservation Award in 2021 and was named Wigan CAMRA Branch Pub of the Year 2026.

Is there a good independent restaurant in Wigan town centre?

Fat Olive on Upper Dicconson Street is a family-run Mediterranean restaurant and bar two minutes from the town centre, led by executive chef Matt Varley. It serves locally sourced Lancashire produce with a southern European flavour, and is open for lunch and dinner. Booking ahead for evenings is recommended.

Can you do a Wigan food day on foot and public transport?

Mostly. The Swan and Railway and Fat Olive are walkable from both Wigan North Western and Wigan Wallgate stations, which are minutes apart and well connected to Manchester and Liverpool. Whittles Bakery in Pemberton and Mayhap Coffee in Tyldesley are a short bus ride away, easily combined into a full-day food tour of the borough.
