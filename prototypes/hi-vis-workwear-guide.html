The short version

Two materials, three classes, one decision

High-visibility clothing works two ways at once. Fluorescent background fabric — the bright yellow or orange — lifts the wearer out of the background in daylight and dusk. Retroreflective tape bounces vehicle headlights straight back at the driver after dark. A compliant garment needs enough of both, and the standard that sets "enough" is EN ISO 20471.

That standard sorts garments into three classes by the minimum area of each material they carry. More area means visibility at greater distance and in worse conditions — so the class you need is driven by risk, chiefly the speed of nearby traffic and the light level you work in. The rest of this hub walks through the classes, how to choose, and where hi-vis is effectively mandatory.

At a glance

The three classes

Class is set by the minimum area of fluorescent background and retroreflective material on the garment. Higher class = seen from further away.

Lowest coverage

Class 1

≈ 0.14 m² fluoro · 0.10 m² tape

Low-risk, off-road settings — car parks, warehouses away from traffic. Often trousers or a light-duty band set.

Mid coverage

Class 2

≈ 0.50 m² fluoro · 0.13 m² tape

The everyday workhorse — typically a waistcoat or vest. Busy yards, deliveries, moderate-speed environments.

Highest coverage

Class 3

≈ 0.80 m² fluoro · 0.20 m² tape

Maximum visibility — normally needs sleeves or trouser bands to reach the area. High-speed roads, night work, highways.

The bit people miss

Hi-vis wears out its compliance

A hi-vis garment isn't compliant forever. Fluorescence fades under UV and washing, and retroreflective tape cracks and dulls with wear — so a jacket that still looks yellow can quietly drop below EN ISO 20471. That's why garments are marked with a maximum number of wash cycles, and why faded or damaged kit should be replaced, not trusted.

Questions

Hi-vis FAQ

What are the three hi-vis classes?

EN ISO 20471 defines three classes by the minimum area of fluorescent background and retroreflective material a garment carries. Class 1 is the lowest coverage, Class 2 sits in the middle (typically a waistcoat or vest), and Class 3 is the highest and normally needs sleeves or trouser bands to reach the required area. Higher class means visibility over a greater distance and in worse conditions.

Which hi-vis class do I legally need?

There's no single legal class — it's risk-led. The higher the speed of nearby traffic and the poorer the light, the higher the class. High-speed roads, night work and highway maintenance point to Class 3; busy yards and moderate-speed environments to Class 2; low-risk off-road settings to Class 1. Some sectors set their own rule — rail, for example, mandates orange Class 3.

Does hi-vis stop being compliant over time?

Yes. Fluorescence fades and retroreflective tape degrades with washing, UV and wear, so a faded or damaged garment can drop below the standard even though it still looks yellow. Garments carry a maximum number of wash cycles, and worn kit should be replaced rather than trusted.
