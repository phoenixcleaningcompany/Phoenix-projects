# Phoenix Duct Clean — Marketing & Sales Strategy

Prepared: August 2026

## 1. The numbers, sanity-checked

- Physical capacity: ~5-6 jobs/week ≈ 22-26 jobs/month
- Current volume: 12 jobs/month (of which 8-12 are repeat/rebooking clients — retention is strong)
- Target: 20+ jobs/month, inside existing capacity — **this is a lead-generation problem, not a capacity problem**
- Gap to close: roughly +8 jobs/month, ≈ 96-100/year → consistent with the "100-120 more annual customers" goal
- Current mix: 60% target industries (care homes, nurseries, schools, sports clubs), 40% food service
- Budget: £500 to test paid channels
- Time: ~20 hrs/week for marketing/sales, with heavy AI/automation use

**Root cause of the current shortfall:** phoenixcleaningcompany.com (15 years old) used to generate most new leads. It listed 20+ unrelated services (trauma cleaning, crime scene cleanup, monkeypox sanitising, alongside duct cleaning) with thin content on each — a pattern Google's core/spam updates over 2022-2024 specifically demote. That's almost certainly why leads dried up, not bad luck. The business has been surviving on repeat bookings since. phoenixductclean.com (4 weeks old, narrow focus) is the right long-term fix, but a brand-new domain realistically needs 6-12+ months to rank for competitive commercial terms. **Near-term growth has to come from channels other than organic search.**

## 2. Priority order (highest ROI for the time/money available, first)

### Pillar 1 — Fix the foundation (free, do first, prevents self-sabotage)
1. Consolidate the two websites: redirect phoenixcleaningcompany.com into phoenixductclean.com. You no longer do trauma/biohazard work, so the old site is dead weight that confuses Google and any visitor who lands on it. Redirecting passes along 15 years of accumulated domain trust instead of starting from zero.
2. Consolidate to a single Google Business Profile under the new site/brand. Two GBPs at the same address/phone risks one being suppressed as a duplicate, and splits any trust signal you build.
3. Trim the "industries served" list on the new site. It currently includes takeaways, nightclubs, and implicitly heavy-use pub chains — exactly the enquiries you don't want. Narrowing it to care homes, nurseries, schools, sports clubs, hotels, and moderate food service both filters out time-wasting enquiries and sharpens what Google understands you're relevant for.
4. Add a rough price guide to the site (e.g. "kitchen extraction cleaning from £550", "LEV testing from £500+VAT per site"). People searching for cost and finding nothing often just leave. Ranges pre-qualify enquiries and cut down on calls that go nowhere.
5. Add a simple contact form — phone/email-only raises friction for anyone who'd rather not call.
6. Ask for 5-10 Google reviews from your longest-standing, lowest-stigma relationships — schools and care homes, not restaurants. A handful of genuine reviews meaningfully improves trust and visibility once the GBP is consolidated.

### Pillar 2 — Reactivate the existing client base (near-zero cost, highest confidence)
Retention is already strong (8-12 of 12 monthly jobs are rebookings), which means people who've used you once tend to stay. That makes your 15-year email history the single best asset you have.
1. Mine the scattered email threads into a structured list: client name, site, industry, last clean date, service type. This is genuinely automatable — export or grant access to the mailbox and Claude Code can extract and structure it. Say the word and I'll start on this.
2. Segment the list: (a) lapsed target-industry clients who haven't rebooked in 12-24 months, (b) active clients (ask for referrals + reviews), (c) lapsed acceptable food-service clients (lower priority).
3. Run a simple, factual re-engagement message to group (a): "It's been [X] months since your last TR19 clean/LEV test — you may be due your compliance renewal." Not salesy, just a nudge from someone they already trust.

### Pillar 3 — Build the facilities/block management channel deliberately (highest long-term leverage)
Your two biggest wins — 6 schools via one facilities management company, 10 laundry sites via one block manager — both came in by accident, via old-site leads that no longer arrive. Nobody had ever gone and deliberately built more of these relationships until this round of work (see Section 4 below, which covers what's actually been built).
1. ~~Build a target list~~ — done, four lists totaling 3,141 contacts (Section 4).
2. Approach directly with a compliance-led, portfolio-scheduling pitch — sequences drafted for all four lists (Section 4). Existing client relationships (the 6 schools, the 10 laundry sites) are referenced generically in these ("a facilities management client," "a block management client") rather than named, since naming them hasn't been explicitly cleared.
3. Build reciprocal referral relationships with catering equipment engineers and fire risk assessors — they encounter non-compliant kitchens and LEV systems in their own work and are a natural, no-cost referral source. **Catering engineers/commercial kitchen installers: done** — 531-contact England-wide list built and a referral-partner sequence drafted (Section 4). Fire risk assessors remain unactioned — no directory equivalent found/attempted for that group yet.
4. **Revised from the original plan:** LEV testing was dropped from the executed cold-outreach sequences per your direction — kitchen extraction and laundry duct cleaning are the two services actually being pitched to care homes/nurseries/schools, block management, and facilities management. LEV remains a legally-stronger argument in principle (HSE/COSHH-enforced vs. TR19's insurance-driven angle), so it's worth reintroducing as a specific angle later if the current sequences underperform, or in-person/reply-thread once a conversation starts.

### Pillar 4 — SEO groundwork (slow, cheap, don't expect near-term leads from it)
1. Keep the new site narrow — don't repeat the old site's mistake of chasing every unrelated service.
2. Build a small number of genuinely useful pages rather than many thin ones: a TR19 explainer, a LEV/COSHH legal-requirement explainer, and pages per target industry (care homes, schools/nurseries, student accommodation/laundry) rather than per-location, since you're not a fixed local business.
3. Get listed on relevant trade directories (e.g. NAAC/BESA if applicable, facilities-management supplier directories) — cheap, slow-compounding trust signals.
4. The old-site redirect in Pillar 1 is likely the single highest-value SEO action available, since it transfers 15 years of domain history instead of waiting for a 4-week-old domain to build it from nothing.

### Pillar 5 — £500 paid ad test (last, and narrow)
Generic search terms like "kitchen deep clean" put you in a bidding war against the £400 cowboys and £1500 national players, on clicks that can run £5-15+ each — £500 will evaporate fast with little to show. Better use of the budget:
- Narrow, compliance-intent search terms ("LEV testing requirement care home", "COSHH LEV test") — lower volume, but the searcher is legally motivated rather than price-shopping.
- Or redirect the £500 toward LinkedIn outreach credits targeting facilities/property managers directly, which fits Pillar 3 better than broad search ads.
- Treat this as a small, deliberate test, not the main growth engine — Pillars 2 and 3 are higher-confidence for the time and money available.

### What didn't work, so it isn't retried later
- **Day nurseries (England):** Ofsted's register has name/address only, no phone/email/website at all — worse than CQC. No bulk source with contact info exists; would need website-discovery-from-zero for ~14,573 providers, a much bigger job than the care home one. Parked, not attempted at scale.
- **Restaurants/pubs/cafes (general hospitality):** No free UK equivalent of the care regulators exists for this vertical. FHRS (the food hygiene ratings body) has no contact fields at all; daynurseries.co.uk-style consumer directories (Yell, daynurseries.co.uk itself) are bot-protected; WhatPub/CAMRA is accessible but has no contact fields either. The realistic path for hospitality specifically is a commercial list broker (Prospect360, RD Marketing, etc. — likely the same category of provider behind the successful golf club email list from March), paid for out of the £500 test budget, not a free scrape.
- **Chain/independent split:** applied to the two care home lists (filtering out large corporate chains and local authorities, since they need the facilities-manager relationship motion, not a mass individual-site email) but not to block management/facilities management, since those lists were already small enough (133 and 73) not to need it.

### Sending caution (applies to all four lists)
- Test a small batch first (a few hundred, not the full list at once) before scaling — same logic as the rugby club test that got 2 bookings from 500 emails.
- Mailchimp's acceptable-use policy is built for opt-in permission marketing and has been known to flag/suspend accounts for cold lists sourced from directories — test a small batch through their review before committing further, or use a tool built for cold outreach instead (Instantly, Lemlist, Smartlead) or manual sending via Gmail/Yahoo (confirmed comfortable at ~300/day across your business accounts without spam issues).
- Stop a sequence for anyone the moment they reply, quote, or ask to be left alone.
