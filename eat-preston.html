Where to Eat in Preston

Four moods, one proud Lancashire city.

The Four Picks

Takeaway/Casual

Lahori Xpress

Unit 1, 6-8 Terrace Street, Preston city centre

Lahori Xpress opened in 2022 and rapidly became one of Preston's most talked-about South Asian kitchens, rated 4.8 out of 5 on Google across more than 200 reviews. The menu is rooted in Lahori cooking: heavy iron karahi dishes cooked down to a rich sauce and served sizzling in the pan, tandoori bread baked fresh to scoop it up, and specials built around slow-cooked lamb and chicken. The chicken karahi and lamb biryani draw the most praise, and the kitchen holds a 5-star food hygiene rating (confirmed September 2025). Takeaway and dine-in, available on Uber Eats and Just Eat, open five days a week from the evening. Preston's Pakistani community has been feeding the city for decades, and Lahori Xpress is the sharp end of that tradition.

Cafe

Cafune

35 Market Place (Flag Market), Preston city centre

Cafune has a claim no other UK cafe can match: it is the first South American coffee shop in the country. Founded by Livia and Sean, who met while teaching English in Ecuador, the cafe sits on Preston's Flag Market and takes the street food of South America entirely seriously. Colombian espresso is the backbone, alongside Argentine cappuccino and Brazilian guarana fizz hard to find anywhere else in England. The food follows the same logic: Brazilian custard cake, La Fantastica vegan cake, empanadas and South American-themed brunch plates. Reviews from 2025 and early 2026 rate it at 4.8 on Google; Restaurant Guru ranked it 16th of 689 Preston restaurants as of March 2026. A small outside terrace, cosy interior and a staff genuinely passionate about what they serve make it something Preston is quietly proud of.

Restaurant

Aven

10-11 Camden Place, Winckley Square, Preston city centre

Aven opened in October 2023 in a Georgian townhouse beside Winckley Square, named for the neighbouring Avenham Park, and in under three years has become Preston's most decorated restaurant. Chef-patron Oli Martin and head chef Sean Wrest run a 32-cover dining room offering five- and eight-course tasting menus built entirely on Lancashire seasonal and foraged produce. The cooking has won three AA Rosettes, a listing in the 2025 Michelin Guide as 'Good cooking', and in 2026 a place at number 75 in SquareMeal's Top 100 UK restaurants outside London. Signature dishes have included a butter pie reimagined with truffled dauphinoise and Lancashire cheese custard, and a duck leg suet pudding delivering intense flavour. Wednesday to Saturday, lunch and dinner; book well ahead.

Pub

The Black Horse

166 Friargate, Preston city centre

There has been a pub called the Black Horse on Friargate since 1739, and the building that stands today was constructed in 1898 by architect J. A. Seward for the Atlas Brewery Company. It is Grade II listed and sits on CAMRA's National Inventory of Historic Pub Interiors, a distinction shared by fewer than 200 pubs in the country. The interior is remarkable: a mosaic floor bearing the words THE BLACK HORSE in the entrance vestibule, ceramic wall tiles, one of only 14 surviving ceramic bar counters in England, stained glass and fixed seating. Ten handpumps serve four Robinsons ales and four rotating guests. Landlord Dan Taylor has steered it to CAMRA's Central Lancashire Pub of the Year for three consecutive years (2023, 2024, 2025) and into the national CAMRA final four in 2025.

Preston's Food Scene

Preston gained city status in 2002 and its food scene has been making a quiet case for that title ever since. The city centre clusters around the Flag Market and Fishergate, where independents have moved into Victorian arcades and Georgian townhouses, while Friargate - the long medieval street running north from the market - holds the oldest pubs and some of the best street-level browsing in Lancashire. Winckley Square, the elegant Georgian set-piece to the south of the centre, has become the address for the city's fine-dining ambitions, anchored by Aven.

Preston's South Asian communities, predominantly Pakistani and Bangladeshi, arrived from the 1950s and 1960s and have shaped the city's everyday eating as thoroughly as anywhere in the North West. The city has a concentration of halal karahi and tandoori kitchens that draws diners from across Lancashire, and Lahori Xpress on Terrace Street is the current standard-bearer. The butter pie, Preston's own contribution to the national pie canon, was born here in the nineteenth century as a meatless Friday dish: potato, onion and butter in short pastry, sold from bakeries, chip shops and market stalls across the city, and still served on match days at Deepdale Stadium.

The Flag Market and the indoor market on Earl Street are the natural heart of Preston's independent food culture, with stalls selling local produce alongside the cafes and takeaways that ring the square. Cafune, planted on the Flag Market itself, represents the city's appetite for the genuinely new: a South American cafe that has no equal in the UK. The Black Horse on Friargate is the other pole - a pub whose Victorian bones have survived almost unchanged for 125 years and whose cask ale programme is among the best in Lancashire.

Behind the Pass: The Kitchens That Keep Preston Fed

Every venue in this guide runs on a commercial kitchen, and behind each cookline sits an extraction system - canopy, baffle filters, plenum, ductwork and roof fan - pulling heat, smoke and grease-laden vapour out of the building. Preston's kitchens run the full spectrum: late-night Pakistani karahi houses firing heavy iron karahis at fierce heat, a fine-dining room turning out multi-course tasting menus, and a Victorian pub pouring cask ale under tiled ceilings all push grease-laden vapour into canopies and ductwork every service across the city.

Under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, the "responsible person" for a premises must assess and manage fire risk, and a grease-laden duct is one of the most serious risks in any catering building. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) reinforce the same duty of care, while an Environmental Health Officer (EHO) or a buildings insurer can ask to see documented proof the extraction system has been professionally cleaned.

What is TR19 Grease?

TR19 Grease is the UK industry standard for cleaning ductwork and extraction systems in commercial kitchens, published by the Building Engineering Services Association (BESA). It defines how grease deposits are measured, the maximum thickness allowed before cleaning is due, and the post-clean certification a kitchen should hold - the benchmark insurers, fire assessors and EHOs look for.

The food gets the headlines. The extraction system is what keeps the kitchen open, insured and safe - quietly, behind the pass.

Industry Insight

The Cleaning Frequency Most Operators Get Wrong

Here is something many venue owners do not realise: under TR19 Grease, cleaning frequency is set by how many hours the kitchen actually cooks, not by how the canopy looks. A heavy-use kitchen running 12 to 16 hours a day should be cleaned roughly every three months. Moderate use of 6 to 12 hours points to every six months, and light use of 2 to 6 hours to every twelve.

The catch is that baffle filters can look clean while the plenum, the horizontal duct runs and the fan housing carry a heavy grease load that throttles airflow and lowers extract efficiency. A fire in that concealed ductwork spreads fast. That is why a dated TR19 certificate, kept in the fire logbook, is the document an insurer expects to see - and its absence at the point of a claim is a common reason cover is refused.

Planning Your Visit

The city centre is compact. Cafune on the Flag Market, the Black Horse on Friargate, Aven on Camden Place and the indoor market on Earl Street all sit within ten minutes on foot. Lahori Xpress on Terrace Street is a short walk east of the market, near Preston bus station. Preston train station is five minutes from the Flag Market on foot, with direct services to Manchester, Liverpool, Blackpool and London.

A full day flows well: a morning coffee at Cafune with a Brazilian bake, a wander through the market and along Fishergate, an afternoon pint at the Black Horse to take in the tiles, an evening karahi at Lahori Xpress, or Aven for a special occasion saved for the evening. Book Aven several weeks ahead - it is a 32-cover room and fills quickly. Parking is in the St John's or Ringway multi-storeys off Fishergate.

  • Start at Cafune on the Flag Market for a Colombian espresso and a South American bake
  • Walk the length of Friargate and call into the Black Horse to see the tiled interior
  • Visit the indoor market on Earl Street for butter pies and local produce
  • Book Aven on Camden Place well ahead - it is Preston's Michelin Guide table
  • Lahori Xpress on Terrace Street opens evenings - the chicken karahi is the order to make

What to Order and How to Do the Day

Order with intent. At Cafune, a Colombian espresso and the Brazilian custard cake - there is nowhere else in England to eat this combination. At Lahori Xpress, the chicken karahi or lamb biryani, scooped up with fresh tandoori bread. At Aven, surrender to the eight-course tasting menu and ask what is foraged and seasonal that week. At the Black Horse, a pint of Robinsons Dizzy Blonde or one of the rotating guests, taken slowly at the ceramic bar counter and looking up at the tiled walls.

Frequently Asked Questions

Preston Food and Drink FAQ

What is the butter pie and where can I get one in Preston?

The butter pie is a Lancashire original born in Preston in the nineteenth century: short pastry filled with potato, onion and butter, made as a meatless Friday dish for Catholic workers. It is sold in Preston bakeries, chip shops and the indoor market on Earl Street, and served on match days at Deepdale Stadium. Aven restaurant has also put a fine-dining version on its tasting menu.

Where can I eat authentic Pakistani food in Preston?

Lahori Xpress at Unit 1, 6-8 Terrace Street is the current standout, with a 4.8 Google rating and a 5-star food hygiene score confirmed September 2025. The kitchen specialises in Lahori karahi dishes, tandoori bread and lamb biryani, open five evenings a week for dine-in and takeaway.

Does Preston have a Michelin-listed restaurant?

Yes. Aven on Camden Place by Winckley Square is listed in the 2025 Michelin Guide as good cooking and holds three AA Rosettes. It was ranked 75th in SquareMeal's Top 100 UK restaurants outside London in 2026. Chef-patron Oli Martin runs seasonal five- and eight-course tasting menus using Lancashire produce and foraged ingredients. Open Wednesday to Saturday.

What makes the Black Horse pub in Preston special?

The Black Horse at 166 Friargate is Grade II listed and sits on CAMRA's National Inventory of Historic Pub Interiors. Built in 1898, it retains a mosaic entrance floor, ceramic wall tiles, one of only 14 surviving ceramic bar counters in England, and original stained glass. It has been named CAMRA Central Lancashire Pub of the Year three years running (2023-2025) and reached the CAMRA UK national final four in 2025.

Where is the best independent coffee in Preston?

Cafune on the Flag Market is the most distinctive - it is the first South American coffee shop in the UK, run by Livia and Sean who travelled South America before opening in Preston. Colombian espresso, Argentine cappuccino and South American bakes are the signatures, rated 4.8 on Google and ranked 16th of 689 Preston restaurants in March 2026.

Can I visit all four Preston food spots in one day?

Easily. Cafune on the Flag Market, the Black Horse on Friargate and Lahori Xpress on Terrace Street are all within ten minutes on foot in the city centre. Aven on Camden Place is a short walk south near Winckley Square. Preston train station is five minutes from the Flag Market, with direct services to Manchester, Liverpool and Blackpool.
